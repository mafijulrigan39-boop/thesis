# Master literature synthesis — Bangladesh's prospective EU market-access exposure

**Research title:** Assessing Bangladesh’s Post-LDC Graduation Market-Access Exposure in the European Union: A Product-Level Analysis of Tariff Preference Erosion and Export Competitiveness.

**Status:** thesis pre-writing synthesis, 21 November 2026. Evidence from 33 screened papers, SciSpace reviewed/selected abstracts and 15 structured method/result extractions, multiple Wiley full-text passage searches, Consensus abstracts for four papers, three citation audit batches plus DOI corrections, and official EU/UN/Eurostat sources. **Not all complete article full texts were read**. All publication claims must be checked at their supporting journal/official text before final submission.

## Executive synthesis — the ten defensible takeaways

1. The economic question is not whether Bangladesh loses a fictional uniform 12% advantage on all exports; it is which product lines face what *conditional, legally applicable, utilized, origin-qualified, competing-supplier-adjusted* tariff advantage upon the eventual EBA transition.
2. **LDC graduation versus EU preference withdrawal is now a different time horizon.** UN resolution A/RES/81/8 adopted 29 October 2026 changes Bangladesh's scheduled graduation to 24 November 2029. Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 provides a further three-year EBA transition after LDC status loss; the earliest nominal prospective loss around November 2032 is conditional on later EU delegated/implementing decisions and subsequent law. Older EU Q&A pages still mention 2026 graduation; they predate that UN decision.
3. Literature demonstrates nontrivial **utilisation** gaps. Sytsma (2021, 2022) and Bekkers & Cariola (2025) show actual customs preference use/RoO matter. EU Commission's official 2024 report describes Bangladesh EBA utilisation of about 96% and approximately EUR 19 billion entered preferentially; do not assume 100% of CN8 trade used EBA.
4. Existing EU CN8 preference studies invalidate a claim of methodological first-mover novelty. Cipollina, Laborde Debucquet & Salvatici (2017) already estimated sector-specific gravity responses using disaggregated eight-digit EU imports and explicit preferential margins.
5. Bangladesh graduation-related **modelled trade changes** exist but rely on different assumptions and outcomes. Rahman & Strutt (2024) conduct CGE simulations including tariff and subsidy scenarios; Bekkers & Cariola (2025) conduct a partial equilibrium simulation with preference-use adjustment. Our existing EUR 2.443bn 2026 MFN static duty equivalent is not a modelled trade decline and cannot be compared as if equivalent.
6. **Competitiveness is multidimensional.** Bangladesh’s market share in EU product imports is a factual destination-market outcome; lower customs EUR/kg is a unit value, not quality or technical productivity; tariff margin is a policy cost advantage, not an observed export performance effect. Balassa (1965), French (2017), Gervais (2015), Hallak (2006) require discipline.
7. Historical product trends must use **source-authenticated CN8 concordances**; Van Beveren, Bernard & Vandenbussche (2012), Baumgartner, Srhoj & Walde (2023), and Eurostat methodology show why yearly changes in codes can mechanically create growth/disappearance.
8. Statistical significance in a deliberately selected top-200 high-value sample is descriptive/exploratory, not representative causal inference. The current weak pooled H2 rank correlation (rho=0.189, p=0.0087) disappears after HS2 adjustment (rho about -0.0096, p=0.843).
9. Article 33's **37% GSP-beneficiary denominator** and **6% all-Union-imports exception** are distinct; the 2027 beneficiary-roster 2023–2025 historical screens are illustrative only and the EBA exception bars current application. Commission implementing acts and applicable legal year are necessary for any future claim.
10. The viable research contribution is a replicable **Bangladesh EU CN8 product-by-competitor exposure map under source-dated scenarios**, with explicit coverage/missingness and legal transition analysis, not a uniquely causal estimate or a fully harmonized 2012–2025 product panel.

## 1. Theoretical foundations and causal mechanisms

### 1.1 Comparative advantage and the meaning of export competitiveness

Balassa (1965, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9957.1965.tb00050.x) popularized revealed comparative advantage (RCA) as a trade-share index. France's [CORRECTION: French, Scott] published article (2017, DOI 10.1016/j.jinteco.2017.02.002) shows that different RCA measures answer different questions and do not generically equal productivity. For a Bangladesh-only EU import-market dataset, the **EU product market share** is defensible, but classic Balassa RCA against total world exports is **not directly computable** without a consistent world exporter×product benchmark.

Krugman (1980) explains differentiated products and increasing returns, while Melitz (2003, DOI 10.1111/1468-0262.00467) makes exporter productivity heterogeneity central to export participation. These supply plausible mechanisms, not variables or identified firm effects in country-level Comext data. Avoid claiming the research directly measures firm productivity, domestic value added, unit labour cost, buyer relationships, or profitability.

### 1.2 Tariff preference margins and competing suppliers

Preferential treatment changes relative EU-landed trade costs only when the good and exporter actually **qualify** and **claim** those preferences. Several different margins should be distinguished:

- **Legal preference benefit:** the applicable MFN duty minus Bangladesh's eligible preference rate for the same product and reference date.
- **Competitor-relative tariff advantage:** competitor origin's legally qualified product tariff less Bangladesh's qualified duty, with the same import regime and date.
- **Effective/used preference advantage:** the rate advantage weighted by actual utilisation or transactions claiming preferential origin, rather than assuming all eligible lines used a certificate.
- **Graduation erosion scenario:** a potential *change* in Bangladesh-origin applicable duty after loss of EBA, conditional on access to Standard GSP, GSP+, an FTA, no preferences, rules of origin, and product safeguard treatment.

Cipollina et al. (2017, DOI 10.1007/s10290-016-0270-0) show estimated preferences and trade elasticities vary by sector and the reference tariff used. Raimondi, Scoppola & Olper (2012, DOI 10.1007/s10290-012-0134-1) show in rice, quotas and margin definitions change the gravity results. Borchert (2009, DOI 10.1111/j.1540-5982.2009.01549.x) documents preference-induced trade diversion: Bangladesh's future competitive position is about the *relative* advantages among China, India, Vietnam, Pakistan, Cambodia, Türkiye and other suppliers, not just Bangladesh's own MFN tariff.

### 1.3 Rules of origin, utilisation and value-chain effects

Sytsma (2021, DOI 10.1111/coep.12529) uses a triple-difference framework for the EU's 2011 apparel rules-of-origin revision and finds that sourcing requirements inhibit actual preference use. His firm-level Bangladesh follow-up (2022, DOI 10.1257/pol.20200257) analyzes transaction-level exporter adjustment, including shipment sizes and quality-upgrading channels. This directly disproves an assumption that preferences are costless or automatically claimed.

Curran & Nadvi (2015, DOI 10.1093/cjres/rsv019) relate the altered EU rules to garment-chain knit/woven differences and restructuring; the available data do not prove gains were shared among workers or translated into upgrading. Tanaka (2021, DOI 10.1111/twec.13108) applies difference-in-differences to Cambodia's 2011 reforms. He reports a substantial garment-export response and changed sourcing, but that was a **specific realized intervention** in Cambodia, not proof that Bangladesh's prospective 2030s EBA exit will cause the same effect.

The governance framework of Gereffi, Humphrey & Sturgeon (2005, DOI 10.1080/09692290500049805) supplies context for why bargaining, buyer sourcing standards and imported fabrics mediate the effects of preference margins. Do not present these as measured mediating variables unless the thesis obtains firm/buyer observations.

### 1.4 Gravity, equilibrium modeling and what it takes to estimate effects

Anderson & van Wincoop (2003, DOI 10.1257/000282803321455214) emphasize multilateral resistance in structurally consistent gravity. Santos Silva & Tenreyro (2006, DOI 10.1162/rest.88.4.641) address heteroskedasticity problems in log-linear gravity and motivate PPML for many trade-flow datasets. Melitz and other heterogeneous-firm models provide microfoundations for exporter selection.

However, a one-year destination-importer 2025 cohort of 200 *selected* Bangladesh products plus six competitors, and a 2026 **source tariff snapshot**, has no separately observed tariff change, untreated/treated units and post-event outcome. Running cross-sectional OLS or PPML mechanically on these data cannot identify the causal response to a 2029/2032 event. Difference-in-differences requires genuine policy implementation, defined treated/comparison products, an appropriately timed preperiod, and credible parallel-trend and spillover checks; **do not invent such a design**.

Rahman & Strutt (2024, DOI 10.1080/09535314.2022.2138271) are an important Bangladesh CGE comparator: their GDP/export changes are outcomes from a calibrated multi-shock equilibrium model, not administrative tariff bills. Bekkers & Cariola (2025, DOI 10.1080/09638199.2024.2323661) model graduated and other LDC exporters in partial equilibrium and explicitly compare actual versus full utilisation. Their published model reports that assuming full use overstates total adverse graduated-LDC export effects by approximately 30%, **in that study's model and sample**. This is not a universal 30% adjustment to Bangladesh's 2025 tariff bill.

A credible second-stage trade-loss estimate would require product elasticity estimates, exporter/importer tariff incidence, customer demand, pass-through assumptions, actual utilisation, future applicable rates, country-substitution effects and confidence/bounds. Without these, an accounting burden is labelled a *static counterfactual duty equivalent*.

## 2. Empirical literature: convergence, disagreements and research gaps

### 2.1 Foundational impact of preferences: heterogeneous rather than universal

Brenton (2003, World Bank Working Paper DOI 10.1596/1813-9450-3018) demonstrates that EBA's incremental benefits depend on what the destination already imports MFN-duty-free and compliance costs. This finding matters when some of Bangladesh's product lines have zero MFN rates even before EBA.

Cirera, Foliano & Gasiorek (2011, indexed working-paper record; bibliographic manifestation pending) estimate product-level EU GSP gravity models and incorporate whether preferences actually enter under eligible regimes; the reported gains are generally small at the intensive margin and sensitive to the extensive margin and preference metric. Nilsson (2011, DOI 10.1111/j.1813-6982.2011.01309.x) finds low-value 8-digit customs flows often use preferences less than aggregates imply. These form a methodological argument for a **product/partner-specific preference-utilisation dataset** instead of assigning the national annual 96% rate to each CN8 item without qualification.

Cipollina et al. (2017) find the size of the preferential tariff margin does not mechanically translate to proportionate changes in trade volumes across sectors. Borchert (2009) shows the hierarchy of preferences can divert exports toward favored partners; preferences are relative trade barriers.

### 2.2 Bangladesh-specific literature and what it does not establish

- **Sytsma 2021 and 2022:** strongest Bangladesh sourcing-rule and revealed preference-use mechanisms, with credible treatment-related variation around **2011**. Relevance: distinguish actual certificate/rule from legal headline tariffs; not a 2032 projection.
- **Curran & Nadvi 2015:** highlights EU origin rules and Bangladeshi textiles/apparel value-chain change. Relevance: interpret why access can alter upgrading/incentives, but not make a causal labour-market claim.
- **Rahman & Strutt 2024:** Bangladesh CGE simulations. Relevance: compare macroeconomic scenarios, not direct numerical equality with tariff cost accounting.
- **Bekkers & Cariola 2025:** simulation for multiple graduating LDCs with actual-vs-full utilisation. Relevance: make rates conditional and refrain from automatically assuming 100% usage.
- **Tanaka 2022 (DOI 10.1111/twec.13329):** Cambodia's realized partial EU preference withdrawal is a quasi-experimental analogue. Relevance: candidate external validation or future comparator, not Bangladesh outcomes.

### 2.3 Opposing perspectives that a credible discussion must present

**Preference can aid exports:** the Sytsma/Tanaka reforms show easier rules can raise use or exports; some EU trade-gravity estimates find favorable trade effects. **But effectiveness is not homogeneous:** Cirera and Cipollina find modest or specification-dependent effects, Nilsson shows low utilisation for small lines, and Brenton finds sectors already MFN free have little extra legal benefit. **Macro shock forecasts are model-dependent:** Rahman & Strutt and Bekkers & Cariola have different counterfactual structures, tariff pathways and parameters. Our research should compare the assumptions first, not present one forecast as consensus.

## 3. Export competitiveness measurement literature

### 3.1 EU product market share versus full revealed comparative advantage

For CN8 product p in 2025, observed EU extra-EU market share is Bangladesh-origin EU imports in product p divided by EU imports from **all extra-EU origins** in product p. This is a product destination-market penetration indicator. Do not call it Balassa RCA (which would require country/world total trade shares), underlying productivity or durable competitive advantage. If a verified world benchmark later becomes available, calculate RCA separately and transparently using a defined year and consistent HS product level.

### 3.2 Concentration and supply dependence

A top-200 basket concentration HHI or cumulative top-10 share uses Bangladesh product export values as weights. A supplier-origin HHI for one EU product market requires all country origins, not just six preferred competitors. Where origin totals do not add up to Eurostat's published extra-EU total, label the partial HHI **provisional** and calculate sensitivity bounds rather than replacing residual with zero.

Concentration at CN8 and HS2 measures different objects: a low average HS2 dispersion does not prove low risk if products within that HS2 all share the same tariff or destination. Discuss aggregation sensitivity.

### 3.3 Product-level unit values: useful, not quality

Customs unit value in euros per kilogram is observed import value divided by positive, valid net mass for a matched CN8+year+origin. Two suppliers can differ in their quality mix, transport cost, customer contracting, pricing-to-market or product composition within the same eight-digit category. Szczygielski & Grabowski (2009, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.1517756), Gervais (2015, DOI 10.1111/caje.12171), Hallak (2006, DOI 10.1016/j.jinteco.2005.04.001), Hummels & Klenow (2005, DOI 10.1257/0002828054201396), and Khandelwal (2010, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-937X.2010.00602.x) illustrate why the quality/productivity interpretation requires additional identification, not a raw EUR/kg rank.

The EU dataset's quantities can be affected by suppressed observations and different supplementary units: maintain separate net-mass and supplementary-count analyses; never convert counts/number of pairs to kilograms arbitrarily.

### 3.4 Constant market share analysis and why it is currently blocked

Ahmadi-Esfahani (2006, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8489.2006.00364.x) cautions that constant market share decompositions depend on reference/aggregation assumptions. Without verified common-code 2012–2025 lineages, a product-by-product historical CMSA would mistake EU code revisions for growth or shifts. HS2 aggregate chapter trends are admissible but cannot solve CN8 harmonisation.

## 4. Data methodology literature: classification and annual comparability

Van Beveren, Bernard & Vandenbussche (2012, DOI 10.2139/ssrn.2185895) create EU CN8 trade/prodcom concordance procedures; Baumgartner, Srhoj & Walde (2023, DOI 10.1515/jbnst-2022-0034) show the necessity of consistent product classifications over time. Pierce & Schott (2012) provide a methodologically related US HS-code genealogy, not an out-of-the-box EU concordance.

Eurostat's detailed ITGS metadata explicitly says **EU extra-EU import partners are recorded by origin**, that import and export mirror values may differ due to CIF/FOB and other statistical conventions, and that the CN changes annually. In 2012 alone Eurostat lists 907 CN creations and 818 deletions (national/scope-independent metadata example). Consequently:
- Unit of analysis should be named EU import flow, origin, year, current year's CN8 meaning, value, mass and source vintages.
- A 200-by-14-year *grid* is not 200 valid product-year observations. Task G observed 187 valid rows in 2012, 199 in 2024, 200 in 2025 within its fixed 2025 code grid.
- Missing country/product import record is **not automatically zero**.
- Correspondence sets may be 1:1, split, merge or complex; use conservative families and reject unsupported growth.

## 5. Legal mechanism and how it changes the thesis's time dimension

UNGA Resolution A/RES/81/8 on 29 October 2026 authoritatively extended Bangladesh's preparatory period to **24 November 2029**. The 2026 EU GSP legislation (Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, commencing 1 January 2027) retains an additional **three-year** EBA transition after graduation. Subject to subsequent EU delegated acts and no policy revision, the prospective EBA-exit horizon now lies **around 2032**, not 2029. Some older 2026 EU explanatory webpages still say 'Bangladesh graduates in 2026'; those texts are now stale for national scheduling.

Article 33 distinguishes the percentage of HS2 61–63 GSP section S-11b imports from **all GSP beneficiary countries**, with a **37%** threshold in Annex IV, from a separate **6%** de minimis exception using total Union imports of those same goods. While Bangladesh is an EBA beneficiary the special withdrawal paragraph does not apply, irrespective of a numerical 2027-roster backcast. A Commission act, future applicable roster and actual schemes matter; a product-rate scenario is neither an automatic safeguard nor a forecast legal tariff.

## 6. The narrow, defensible and publishable research gap

**What has already been done:** EU CN8 product-level gravity for preferences, firm-level Bangladesh origin-rule policy identification, macro or partial-equilibrium LDC graduation cost simulations, and analytical work on CN8 concordance and customs unit values. Do not assert 'no prior product-level or Bangladesh graduation studies'.

**What the current project can contribute:** under a clear 2025 selected-country-product cohort, simultaneously document:
1. trade-value concentration and EU extra-EU product market shares,
2. matched competing-origin customs performance and unit-value proxies,
3. source-dated 2026 numeric tariff candidates and explicit missing legal qualifications,
4. several *conditional* post-EBA tariff-pathway benchmarks rather than a single unqualified tariff,
5. S-11b Article 33 numerators/denominators and dated roster backcasts,
6. code lineage and origin residual missingness; permutation and sign-test sensitivity where defensible.

The integrating contribution is **transparent decision-ready exposure accounting and reproducibility with legal and statistical caveats** in light of the 2029–2032 horizon. This is an applied trade-policy/data-methods contribution, not a newly identified causal law of tariff loss.

**Publication-quality upgrade still needed:** independent product-level actual preference-use extraction (EU customs/GSP reporting); legal GSP product and country schedule if publication claims current/future applicable duties; true CN8 annual concordances for historical product trends; external destination or longer variation with defensible identifying policy shock if seeking impact estimates. Publishability cannot be guaranteed and peer-review acceptance depends on methodology, novelty, writing, outlet and supervisor review.

## 7. How this literature must enter the mandatory seven-chapter report

- Chapter 1 **Introduction**: global trade preference theory → EU GSP/EBA and country change → Bangladesh export portfolio → documented gap. No own results in research gap.
- Chapter 2 **Literature Review**: four coherent themes (preference theory/use; Bangladesh graduation scenarios; competitiveness metrics; product classification/empirical identification). Contrast studies and limits, not author-by-author descriptions.
- Chapter 3 **Theoretical Framework**: comparative advantage + tariff preference/landed-cost framework, supplemented by firm/GVC mechanisms only where relevant. Simple conceptual diagram with legal tariff regime, measured product outcomes and sector confounding; arrows are hypotheses, not identified causality.
- Chapter 4 **Methods**: explicitly quantitative observational secondary data, purposive top-200 2025 CN8, six competitor origins, published official values, 2026-11-01 tariff candidates, conditional scenario formulas, Task G source hash verification, exploratory post-hoc tests and source limitations.
- Chapter 5 **Results**: descriptive and measured inferential outputs only; no policy judgment.
- Chapter 6 **Discussion**: interpretation and literature confrontation (including non-robust H2 within HS2), credible bounded industry/policy implications, substantive limitations.
- Chapter 7 **Conclusion**: limited evidence-based summary and future research priorities.
- References: APA author-date with separately checked publisher metadata and official law/statistics.
