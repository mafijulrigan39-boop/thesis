# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background of the Study

International trade provides opportunities for countries to expand production, access larger markets, and participate in cross-border networks of specialization. However, the ability to compete in a foreign market depends not only on productive capacity but also on the costs and institutional conditions associated with reaching that market. Import tariffs alter the relative costs of products supplied from different origins, while trade policy arrangements can influence the distribution of market-access advantages across countries. The structural gravity literature identifies bilateral trade costs and conditions in alternative supplying markets as important determinants of international trade patterns (Anderson & van Wincoop, 2003). For developing economies whose export industries depend heavily on a limited number of overseas markets, the terms of preferential market access are therefore an important component of their external trading environment.

Non-reciprocal trade preferences have long formed part of the international trade and development framework. Under such arrangements, preference-granting economies offer eligible developing-country exports reduced or zero import duties without requiring equivalent concessions in return. Their rationale is to improve market-access opportunities for economies facing structural development constraints. Nevertheless, the economic value of a preference depends on the tariff that would otherwise apply, the products covered, and the administrative conditions for claiming the benefit. Brenton (2003) demonstrated that the usefulness of the European Union's (EU) Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement varies across least developed countries (LDCs), partly because some products already enter the EU at zero most-favoured-nation (MFN) duties and because compliance with origin requirements may limit use of available preferences.

The concept of *tariff preference erosion* refers to a weakening of the relative tariff advantage enjoyed by a preferred exporter. Such erosion can arise through the removal or modification of a beneficiary's preferential status, reductions in MFN tariffs that narrow the difference between preferential and standard rates, or improvements in the preferential access available to competing suppliers. Consequently, the commercial significance of tariff preferences cannot be assessed by considering a beneficiary country's duty rate in isolation. It also depends on the tariff treatment of competing origins and on the sensitivity of trade to differences in effective market-access costs. Research using detailed EU import data has shown that estimated effects of preferential trade policies vary across sectors and are sensitive to how the relevant preference margins are defined (Cipollina et al., 2017).

The EU provides a particularly important institutional setting in which to examine these issues. Its Generalised Scheme of Preferences (GSP) differentiates between Standard GSP, the special incentive arrangement for sustainable development and good governance (GSP+), and EBA for countries classified by the United Nations as LDCs. EBA offers duty-free and quota-free access to the EU market for eligible products other than arms and ammunition, subject to the scheme's applicable conditions, including rules of origin. In contrast, the tariff treatment available through other GSP arrangements is conditional on the relevant product coverage, beneficiary eligibility, and regulatory requirements. The EU adopted Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 to govern its revised preferential framework from 1 January 2027, maintaining the distinction between these arrangements while updating the applicable institutional rules (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

Importantly, preferential eligibility does not necessarily mean that an exporter obtains preferential treatment in every transaction. Rules of origin determine whether the production and sourcing of a product satisfy the conditions attached to the preference, while documentary and administrative requirements affect whether the preference is claimed. Evidence from the EU's 2011 reform of origin rules for LDC apparel exports demonstrates that such conditions can materially influence preference utilisation (Sytsma, 2021). Using transaction-level data from Bangladeshi apparel exporters, Sytsma (2022) also documented how more flexible origin rules were associated with changes in exporter performance. These studies establish an important distinction between the *nominal availability* of preferential tariffs and their *effective utilisation* in international trade.

The eventual graduation of a country from the UN's LDC category introduces an additional dimension of market-access uncertainty. Although graduation recognises progress against international development criteria, preferences that explicitly depend on LDC status are not necessarily permanent. At the same time, loss of UN LDC classification and withdrawal of EBA treatment do not necessarily occur on the same date. On 29 October 2026, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution A/RES/81/8, extending Bangladesh's preparatory period before graduation until **24 November 2029** (United Nations General Assembly, 2026). Under Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, removal from EBA following loss of LDC eligibility involves a further three-year transitional period and a delegated legal act. Accordingly, if the current timetable and regulatory arrangements remain unchanged, the relevant EU preference-transition horizon would extend approximately to **November 2032**, rather than ending automatically on Bangladesh's UN graduation date. The exact future application date and the replacement tariff regime remain dependent on the legal measures and eligibility conditions in force at that time (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

These international arrangements are especially significant for Bangladesh because its export relationship with the EU is both substantial and concentrated in a limited range of manufactured products. According to the European Commission, bilateral EU–Bangladesh merchandise trade reached approximately **€23.3 billion in 2025**, and textiles accounted for **almost 94% of the value of EU merchandise imports from Bangladesh** in that year (European Commission, n.d.). The importance of preferential access is also evident in customs utilisation data: the European Commission and the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy (2026) reported that approximately **€19 billion** of EU imports from Bangladesh used EBA preferences in **2024**, corresponding to a preference-utilisation rate of **96%** among eligible imports. The distinction between these different statistical indicators is important: total bilateral trade, EU import values, and the value of imports claiming EBA treatment refer to different measures and should not be interpreted interchangeably.

Bangladesh's export dependence on apparel reflects a longer process of integration into international production and sourcing networks. The evolution of its knitwear and woven-garment industries has been influenced by both access to European demand and the specific rules governing preferential origin. Curran and Nadvi (2015) showed how changes in EU origin rules interacted with the sourcing structure and upgrading prospects of Bangladesh's textile and garment value chains. This experience suggests that changes in preferential market access may have implications beyond customs duties alone, potentially shaping purchasing decisions, fabric-sourcing arrangements, and the allocation of activities within production networks. However, those mechanisms need to be distinguished from the observable trade outcomes available in aggregate merchandise statistics.

The prospective change in Bangladesh's EU market-access conditions also raises questions concerning its position relative to suppliers facing different tariff and trade-agreement arrangements. Exporters from Bangladesh do not compete against a single uniform benchmark: competing origins may face MFN duties, GSP-based preferences, bilateral agreement rates, or other product-specific customs conditions. An identical change in Bangladesh's nominal tariff treatment could therefore have differing commercial implications across product categories and competing supply origins. Moreover, competitiveness is multidimensional. Trade-share measures can describe observed patterns of export specialization, but they do not by themselves establish underlying productivity or the causal effect of a tariff preference (Balassa, 1965; French, 2017). A distinction is therefore needed between observed export performance, relative tariff treatment, and the potential consequences of future policy changes.

Existing economic assessments of LDC graduation indicate why this distinction matters. Rahman and Strutt (2024) examined possible consequences of Bangladesh's graduation through a computable general equilibrium framework, while Bekkers and Cariola (2025) analysed graduation-related changes in trade using a partial-equilibrium framework that incorporated actual preference utilisation. Such studies provide valuable evidence on the possible channels through which policy changes may influence trade. Nevertheless, model-based projections depend on their specified tariff scenarios, behavioural assumptions, and other economic conditions. They should not be conflated with the direct arithmetic of applying an alternative tariff rate to an unchanged base of observed imports.

For an assessment of Bangladesh's exposure, the product dimension is consequently important. The value of a preferential arrangement depends on the particular goods exported, the applicable tariff schedule, the extent to which preference conditions are fulfilled, and the market positions of rival suppliers. Broad merchandise or sector-level aggregates may obscure important differences between products within the same industry. The EU's Combined Nomenclature provides detailed eight-digit commodity classifications for reporting merchandise trade by product and origin; Eurostat also notes that these classifications are revised over time, a feature that must be respected when interpreting product-level trade patterns (Eurostat, 2025).

Against this background, Bangladesh's approaching transition from LDC-based preferential access presents a question of **differentiated exposure rather than a uniform and predetermined export loss**. It is necessary to establish how the country's EU export basket is distributed across products, which goods currently benefit from meaningful preferential tariff differences, how competing suppliers are positioned in the same EU product markets, and how alternative legally conditional market-access arrangements could change those differences. Addressing these questions through clearly defined product-level evidence can contribute to a more precise understanding of Bangladesh's prospective EU market-access vulnerability and provide a grounded basis for subsequent analysis of export competitiveness and trade-policy options.

## 1.2 Problem Statement and Research Gap

Bangladesh's prospective transition from least developed country (LDC) status presents a trade-policy challenge because a significant part of its access to the European Union (EU) market is associated with preferences granted under the Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement. The issue is particularly consequential given the composition of bilateral trade: textiles represented almost 94% of the value of EU merchandise imports from Bangladesh in 2025 (European Commission, n.d.). In the preceding year, approximately €19 billion of EU imports from Bangladesh entered under GSP preferences, with an aggregate preference-utilisation rate of 96% (European Commission & High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy, 2026). Although these figures refer to different measures and years, they collectively indicate both the concentration of the export relationship and the practical importance of preferential treatment. The central policy concern is not that graduation will necessarily produce a uniform decline in exports, but that the eventual modification of this treatment could alter the relative market-access conditions faced by Bangladeshi products.

The timing and legal form of that transition make the problem more complex than a straightforward comparison between current duty-free treatment and future most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariffs. The United Nations General Assembly extended Bangladesh's preparatory period for LDC graduation until 24 November 2029 (United Nations General Assembly, 2026). Separately, Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 provides a further three-year transition before an eligible graduating country is removed from EBA through the relevant EU legal procedure (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 17). Consequently, the termination of EBA access, the potential availability of subsequent arrangements such as Standard GSP or GSP+, and the actual duties payable under the future legal framework cannot be treated as predetermined outcomes of the 2029 UN graduation decision. An assessment that applies a single assumed post-graduation tariff rate to all Bangladeshi exports would therefore risk misrepresenting both the timing and the extent of the exposure.

The economic significance of preference erosion also differs across products and competing suppliers. Where the ordinary MFN duty is already zero, duty-free EBA treatment provides no corresponding nominal tariff margin. For products subject to positive MFN duties, the size of the legal advantage depends on the applicable product classification, preferential conditions, and the tariff treatment available to other supplying countries. Brenton (2003) demonstrated that the potential benefits of EBA vary with existing tariff protection and the ability to satisfy origin requirements. Moreover, detailed econometric analysis of EU trade preferences indicates that the measured importance of preferential treatment changes with the definition of the reference tariff and differs substantially between sectors (Cipollina et al., 2017). The relevant exposure for Bangladesh is thus not only the difference between its existing and a hypothetical future tariff, but also the change in its relative position within individual EU product markets.

A further difficulty is the distinction between legally available preferences and those actually used. Origin requirements, sourcing practices, and certification procedures may prevent eligible imports from receiving the preferential rate. Sytsma (2021) found that the EU's 2011 relaxation of rules of origin influenced preference utilisation among LDC apparel exporters, while subsequent transaction-level evidence from Bangladesh connected those changes to exporter adjustment (Sytsma, 2022). Even when a duty difference is clearly established, its economic incidence is uncertain: higher import duties may be reflected in export prices, importer margins, buyer sourcing decisions, or some combination of these channels. Recent Bangladesh-focused work on tariff and exchange-rate pass-through explicitly examines how such adjustments may be distributed between apparel exporters and their EU trading partners (Razzaque et al., 2026). It follows that a nominal tariff calculation, by itself, is neither a direct estimate of export revenue lost nor a measure of the eventual burden borne by Bangladeshi firms.

Previous scholarship has examined important dimensions of this transition. Rahman and Strutt (2024) assessed the potential implications of Bangladesh's LDC graduation using a computable general equilibrium model that considers changes in market access and related policy assumptions. Bekkers and Cariola (2025), employing a partial-equilibrium framework for graduating LDCs, demonstrated the importance of incorporating observed preference utilisation rather than assuming that every eligible transaction claims the available benefit. These studies provide valuable estimates of possible trade and economy-wide adjustments under specified counterfactual conditions. However, their model-based estimates answer a different question from identifying how a contemporary Bangladesh-origin EU import portfolio is distributed across individual tariff lines, how those lines compare with rival origins, and which product-specific tariff assumptions underpin a transparent exposure assessment. Their findings therefore motivate, rather than replace, a complementary examination of product-level exposure.

The existing research also provides substantial evidence on Bangladesh's garment sector and EU preferential trade. Curran and Nadvi (2015) examined how changes in European origin rules interacted with Bangladesh's apparel value chains, while Sytsma (2022) investigated exporter-level responses to preferential market-access reforms. More recently, Razzaque et al. (2026) addressed export-price adjustment in the context of changing tariffs and exchange rates. At the wider EU level, Cipollina et al. (2017) had already employed highly disaggregated eight-digit import data to analyse preferential tariff margins and their heterogeneous trade effects. It would therefore be inaccurate to characterise product-level EU preference analysis, Bangladesh-specific graduation modelling, or research on origin rules as unexplored fields. Rather, the literature reviewed here approaches the issue through different units of analysis, policy periods, outcomes, and modelling assumptions.

The **contextual and empirical gap** relevant to the present study lies in bringing these dimensions together for Bangladesh's current EU export position within a consistently defined product-level framework. A national export total or broad apparel aggregate does not reveal whether market-access exposure is concentrated in a small set of products, how Bangladesh's share of each EU import market differs, or how its nominal tariff position compares with those of major competitors. Similarly, a single Bangladesh-versus-MFN comparison does not establish whether suppliers such as China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, or Türkiye enjoy more or less favourable treatment for the same goods. The studies surveyed provide foundations for analysing these questions, but do not themselves constitute an integrated, source-dated assessment of Bangladesh's leading EU import product lines that simultaneously documents observed market positions, competing-origin comparisons, alternative conditional tariff regimes, and the legal qualifications attached to those regimes.

There is also a **methodological and temporal gap** in how such an assessment should handle classification consistency, incomplete tariff coverage, and uncertainty about future legal status. The EU's eight-digit Combined Nomenclature is revised over time, and scholarship on product-classification harmonisation demonstrates that uncorrected code changes can distort measures of product entry, disappearance, and trade growth (Baumgartner et al., 2023). Likewise, customs unit values may be informative for comparing observed import values relative to quantities, but they should not automatically be interpreted as measures of product quality or exporter productivity (Gervais, 2015). An evidence-based assessment must therefore distinguish observed trade patterns from scenario-dependent tariff accounting, avoid treating missing tariff information as a zero rate, and not infer future trade responses without an identified behavioural model. The revised 2026 EU GSP regulation and Bangladesh's subsequently confirmed 2029 graduation timetable add a further need to date legal assumptions precisely rather than applying superseded transition schedules (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026; United Nations General Assembly, 2026).

Accordingly, this study addresses a **specific integration and measurement gap**, rather than claiming to introduce a previously unknown theory or to identify the causal effects of EBA withdrawal before that policy change has occurred. It seeks to examine Bangladesh's leading product-level export exposure in the EU through observed import-market indicators, comparisons with major supplying countries, and carefully qualified alternative tariff scenarios. The analysis also considers the relevance of the EU's product-specific legal arrangements and the transparency of data coverage and measurement limitations. Its intended contribution is a reproducible basis for distinguishing where potential tariff-preference erosion is concentrated, where competitor-relative conditions may differ, and which uncertainties must be resolved before stronger predictions about post-LDC export adjustment can be made. This narrower evidence-based focus is relevant for assessing Bangladesh's prospective market-access risks without presenting hypothetical duties or statistical associations as realised economic losses.

## 1.3 Research Objectives

The objectives of this study are formulated to address the research problem and empirical gap identified in Section 1.2. They distinguish observed export competitiveness from prospective tariff-preference exposure and focus on outcomes that can be assessed through available trade data, tariff schedules, and relevant EU regulatory provisions.

### 1.3.1 General Objective

The general objective of this study is to assess Bangladesh's prospective market-access exposure in the European Union following its eventual transition from Least Developed Country (LDC)-based trade preferences, through a product-level analysis of tariff preference erosion, export competitiveness, and the relative market positions of major competing suppliers under alternative EU tariff arrangements.

### 1.3.2 Specific Objectives

To achieve the general objective, the study establishes the following specific objectives:

1. **To examine the product-level structure and concentration of EU imports originating in Bangladesh**, using the 200 highest-value Combined Nomenclature eight-digit (CN8) product lines in 2025, with particular emphasis on export values, product and sectoral concentration, and Bangladesh's share of individual EU import markets.

2. **To assess Bangladesh's relative export competitiveness against major supplying countries in the EU market**, namely China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye, by comparing matched product-level import values, EU market shares, and customs unit values where valid and comparable quantity data are available.

3. **To evaluate the distribution of nominal tariff preferences and competitor-relative tariff advantages across Bangladesh's principal export products**, using source-dated EU most-favoured-nation (MFN) and preferential tariff-rate information, while examining sectoral variation, exploratory relationships with market-share indicators, and limitations arising from incomplete or conditional tariff coverage.

4. **To quantify Bangladesh's potential product-level tariff-preference erosion under alternative post-EBA market-access scenarios**, by estimating static tariff-duty equivalents using observed 2025 EU import values and specified tariff-rate assumptions, and assessing the sensitivity of these estimates to product coverage, sectoral composition, classification validity, and missing tariff information.

5. **To examine the potential relevance of the EU's product-specific safeguard provisions to Bangladesh's textile and apparel exports**, particularly the numerical thresholds under Article 33 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, using historical import data and explicitly defined beneficiary-country comparisons while distinguishing illustrative threshold assessments from actual legal applicability.

## 1.4 Research Questions and Hypotheses

The research questions are derived from the general and specific objectives of the study and are framed to distinguish observed export-market characteristics from conditional future market-access exposure. The investigation combines questions that can be addressed through descriptive product-level evidence with a limited set of exploratory statistical hypotheses. This distinction is necessary because the available trade observations precede Bangladesh's eventual withdrawal from the European Union's (EU) Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement; hence, neither observed post-EBA outcomes nor the causal effects of preference withdrawal are available for direct estimation. The interpretation of preferential market access must also account for differences in the magnitude and utilisation of tariff preferences across products and countries (Cipollina et al., 2017; Sytsma, 2021).

### 1.4.1 Research Questions

The study seeks to address the following five research questions.

**RQ1 — Product-level export structure and market position.** How are the value, product concentration, sectoral composition, and EU extra-EU import-market shares of Bangladesh's 200 highest-value CN8 export product lines distributed in the 2025 reference year?

**RQ2 — Competitor-relative export performance.** How does Bangladesh's position in the selected EU product markets compare with that of China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye in terms of matched import values, product-market shares, and customs unit values where comparable quantity observations are available?

**RQ3 — Tariff-preference structure and market-share relationships.** How do source-dated EU MFN tariff-rate candidates and conditional preferential tariff advantages vary across the selected products and competing origins, and what exploratory product-level associations can be observed between those tariff-rate benchmarks and Bangladesh's 2025 EU market shares?

**RQ4 — Conditional tariff-preference erosion.** How do the static tariff-duty equivalents associated with alternative, explicitly specified post-EBA tariff scenarios vary across Bangladesh's principal EU export products, and how sensitive are the calculated exposure measures to sectoral composition, product coverage, code classifications, and missing or legally unqualified tariff observations?

**RQ5 — EU safeguard framework and conditional thresholds.** What do historical EU import data for Harmonized System Chapters 61–63 indicate when compared with the numerical thresholds in Article 33 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 using explicitly stated beneficiary-country assumptions, and how does the EBA exception limit the legal interpretation of these comparisons?

These five questions correspond, respectively, to the five specific objectives in Section 1.3. Questions RQ1, RQ4, and RQ5 principally concern descriptive measurement, accounting identities, and conditional legal assessments; RQ2 and RQ3 additionally motivate exploratory statistical comparisons. In particular, the Article 33 analysis distinguishes the share calculated using imports from the relevant GSP beneficiary countries from the separate 6% exception calculated using all Union imports of the relevant products. A historical calculation that applies a later beneficiary-country roster is a backcast, not a determination that the safeguard was legally applicable during those earlier years (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 33).

### 1.4.2 Exploratory Statistical Hypotheses

To supplement the research questions, the study considers three sets of testable hypotheses. Their purpose is to examine patterns within the selected product cohort, rather than to establish the effect of a future tariff shock. The motivation for distinguishing product sectors and competing suppliers follows research showing heterogeneity in EU tariff-preference effects (Cipollina et al., 2017), while the interpretation of customs unit-value comparisons remains subject to the distinction between recorded price-related measures and underlying product quality or productivity (Gervais, 2015).

**H1 — Sectoral differences in MFN tariff-rate benchmarks (related to RQ3).** Define Δ as the arithmetic mean of the 1 November 2026 EU MFN tariff-rate candidates for selected CN8 products in HS Chapters 61–63 minus the corresponding mean for selected products outside those chapters, using only lines with valid numeric rate candidates.

- **Null hypothesis (H1₀):** Δ ≤ 0. The mean MFN tariff-rate candidate for the selected apparel and related textile chapters is not higher than that for the other selected products.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H1ₐ):** Δ > 0. The mean MFN tariff-rate candidate for the selected apparel and related textile chapters is higher than that for the other selected products.

This directional comparison is assessed through an exploratory one-sided product-label permutation test. It describes differences in the selected tariff schedules; it does not measure the behavioural effect of those duties on trade.

**H2 — Product-level association between tariff rates and EU market shares (related to RQ3).** Let ρ denote the Spearman rank correlation between Bangladesh's 2025 share of extra-EU imports for a selected CN8 product and the product's matched 1 November 2026 EU MFN tariff-rate candidate.

- **Null hypothesis (H2₀):** ρ = 0. No monotonic rank association is detected between the two measured variables in the selected product cohort.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H2ₐ):** ρ ≠ 0. A nonzero monotonic rank association exists between the two measured variables in that cohort.

This two-sided exploratory test does not specify that higher tariffs improve or weaken export performance. The analysis additionally considers sector-composition sensitivity using within-HS2 rank adjustment. Since the tariff-rate candidates and import-market shares refer to different reference dates, the association must not be interpreted as a contemporaneous treatment effect or evidence that future preference erosion causes a change in EU market shares.

**H3 — Relative customs unit values across competing origins (related to RQ2).** For each comparison country *c*—China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye—let πc represent the proportion of valid, non-tied matched 2025 CN8 product observations in which Bangladesh's EU import customs unit value (EUR per kilogram) is lower than that of country *c*.

- **Null hypothesis (H3₀,c):** πc ≤ 0.50. Bangladesh does not exhibit a lower customs unit value in more than half of the valid non-tied product comparisons with origin *c*.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H3ₐ,c):** πc > 0.50. Bangladesh exhibits a lower customs unit value in more than half of the valid non-tied product comparisons with origin *c*.

Six origin-specific exploratory one-sided exact sign tests are considered, excluding ties and observations without valid, positive, comparable net-mass quantities. The analysis addresses multiple testing across these comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure. Relative customs unit values are interpreted only as trade-statistical indicators and not as direct estimates of production costs, export profitability, labour productivity, or product quality (Gervais, 2015).

### 1.4.3 Non-statistical Analytical Assessments

The remaining questions do not require null-hypothesis significance tests. Under **RQ4**, a static tariff-duty equivalent is obtained by applying a clearly specified percentage-point tariff-rate difference to the corresponding observed 2025 product import value and aggregating over product lines with valid, qualified rates. The calculation holds the baseline import values constant. It is therefore an accounting measure, not an estimated reduction in Bangladesh's exports, an actual future customs liability, or a welfare effect. Model-based studies of LDC graduation distinguish such baseline accounting from behavioural trade simulations that depend on substitution and preference-utilisation assumptions (Bekkers & Cariola, 2025).

Under **RQ5**, the relevant import-share ratios are calculated and examined against the numerical provisions of Article 33 and Annex IV of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395. The analysis uses historical observations and specifies when a 2027 beneficiary-country grouping is applied retrospectively. These comparisons do not themselves establish that safeguard duties were imposed, can currently be imposed on an EBA beneficiary, or will necessarily apply after Bangladesh's eventual departure from EBA (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

**Status and interpretation of the hypotheses.** H1–H3 were formulated after the initial examination of the study's underlying trade and tariff data; they are therefore **post-hoc and exploratory**, rather than preregistered confirmatory hypotheses. Moreover, the product cohort is selected purposively by Bangladesh's 2025 EU import value, not drawn randomly from the full universe of exported goods. Statistical significance, if reported, is thus interpreted cautiously and cannot establish population-wide causal relationships. Detailed measurement rules, valid observation counts, test procedures, robustness assessments, and limitations are presented in Chapter 4, while the observed test results are reserved for Chapter 5.

## 1.5 Significance of the Study

Bangladesh's prospective transition from least developed country (LDC)-based preferential market access raises questions that extend beyond the nominal tariff changes associated with graduation. The economic relevance of an eventual change in the European Union's (EU) Everything But Arms (EBA) treatment depends on the product concerned, the preferences available to competing suppliers, and the legal and commercial conditions under which importers use those preferences. The significance of this study lies in developing a more differentiated and verifiable account of that exposure, thereby providing an evidence base for academic interpretation and policy and industry decision-making. Its contribution is primarily analytical and applied: it does not presume that preferential market access will change on a single predetermined date or that an increase in nominal customs duties will translate mechanically into an equivalent reduction in Bangladesh's exports.

### 1.5.1 Theoretical and Academic Significance

The study contributes to the application of international trade theory by examining how preferential market-access arrangements relate to observable patterns of export competitiveness at a disaggregated product level. The literature establishes that the benefits of a tariff preference cannot be inferred from a preferential rate alone: they depend on the relevant reference tariff, differences across competing origins, and the extent to which eligible transactions utilise preferential treatment (Cipollina et al., 2017; Sytsma, 2021). By considering these dimensions together, the study offers a structured application of tariff-preference and relative market-access concepts to Bangladesh's prospective transition from EBA. This is a contribution to the contextual interpretation of established theory rather than a claim to develop a new model of international trade.

The study also helps clarify the relationship between measures of export performance and their theoretical interpretation. A country's observed share of EU imports in a given product category reflects its position within that particular destination market; it is not, on its own, a complete measure of underlying comparative advantage or productivity. French (2017) shows that the interpretation of revealed comparative advantage indicators depends on the question and theoretical setting for which they are used. Distinguishing product-level EU market shares from tariff advantages and customs unit values can therefore help prevent conceptually different indicators from being treated as interchangeable measures of competitiveness. Such clarity is particularly important when a future policy change has not yet occurred and its effects cannot be observed directly.

### 1.5.2 Empirical Significance

Empirically, the study has value in moving beyond national trade totals and broad industry aggregates to examine how prospective market-access exposure is distributed within Bangladesh's principal EU export products. Product-level analysis can identify variation in export concentration, relative EU import-market positions, nominal tariff-rate benchmarks, and comparable trade indicators across competing origins. This disaggregation is relevant because highly aggregated measures can conceal differences among products within the same industry and between suppliers operating under distinct trade arrangements.

The proposed evidence is complementary to, rather than a replacement for, earlier assessments of Bangladesh's LDC graduation. Rahman and Strutt (2024) examined possible economy-wide consequences using a computable general equilibrium framework, while Bekkers and Cariola (2025) evaluated trade adjustments under a partial-equilibrium framework that explicitly considers preference utilisation. These studies address modelled economic responses to specified policy scenarios. By contrast, the present study is intended to establish a transparent picture of the product-level trade values, competitor positions, and conditional tariff differences from which assessments of exposure can be developed. Its empirical contribution lies in that integration and level of detail, not in claiming to estimate an already realised post-EBA adjustment.

### 1.5.3 Methodological Significance

The methodological significance of the research lies in its emphasis on traceable sources, precisely defined indicators, and explicit treatment of uncertainty. The use of EU import statistics permits products and supplying origins to be compared within a common destination-market reporting framework, provided that the underlying product codes, partner definitions, values, and quantities are treated consistently. Eurostat identifies the Combined Nomenclature as the principal classification for detailed EU merchandise trade statistics and notes that its eight-digit codes are subject to annual revisions (Eurostat, 2025). Scholarship on classification harmonisation similarly demonstrates that changes in commodity codes can affect the validity of apparent product-level trends (Baumgartner et al., 2023). A source-dated assessment that documents classification limitations, missing observations, and denominator choices can therefore improve the transparency and reproducibility of applied trade-policy research.

A related methodological contribution is the separation of **static exposure accounting** from **behavioural impact estimation**. Applying an alternative tariff-rate assumption to an observed import value can quantify a conditional duty equivalent, but it does not determine who ultimately bears the tariff, whether demand changes, or how suppliers adjust. Likewise, customs unit values cannot be interpreted automatically as measures of product quality or production efficiency; Gervais (2015) demonstrates why identifying quality requires more than direct unit-value comparisons. By keeping these distinctions visible, documenting the coverage of qualified tariff observations, and treating exploratory statistical tests as noncausal, the study can provide an auditable framework for interpreting what the available data establish and what remains uncertain. Such transparency is particularly relevant when the prospective tariff regime is contingent on future legal and administrative developments.

### 1.5.4 Policy and Institutional Significance

From a policy perspective, the study is relevant to Bangladesh's preparation for changing EU market-access conditions. The United Nations General Assembly (2026) extended Bangladesh's preparatory period for LDC graduation until 24 November 2029; the EU's separate rules governing eventual removal from EBA mean that graduation and the withdrawal of EBA treatment must be examined as distinct legal events (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). At the same time, access under Standard GSP, GSP+, or another arrangement cannot be presumed without reference to the eligibility, origin, and implementation conditions that would apply at the relevant time.

A product-level account of conditional tariff margins may assist institutions responsible for export policy, trade negotiations, customs administration, and export development in identifying which product groups warrant closer monitoring and verification. It can also help distinguish policy questions arising from preferential eligibility and rules of origin from those involving competitiveness within individual EU markets. The analysis of Article 33 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 has a related institutional purpose: separating numerical safeguard thresholds from the legal conditions and procedures required for their application (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). The study does not determine Bangladesh's future eligibility for a particular scheme or whether a safeguard will be imposed; rather, it provides a disciplined basis for evaluating such possibilities as the policy framework evolves.

### 1.5.5 Industry and Broader Socioeconomic Significance

For exporters, manufacturers, sourcing intermediaries, and industry associations, the practical relevance of the study arises from the uneven exposure that may exist across products and competing supplying countries. Information on product-specific EU market positions and conditional tariff differences could support more focused monitoring of market access, sourcing requirements, buyer negotiations, and possible export diversification opportunities. Research on Bangladesh's garment industry shows that trade preferences and origin rules have interacted with sourcing decisions and value-chain development, underscoring the relevance of institutional market-access conditions to firms' commercial environments (Curran & Nadvi, 2015; Sytsma, 2022).

The broader socioeconomic importance of these questions follows from the role that export-oriented production can play in industrial activity and employment. Nevertheless, the present study does not directly measure wages, employment, household welfare, firm profitability, or changes in domestic value added. Its contribution to those wider concerns is therefore indirect: by identifying products for which preferential market-access conditions may deserve particular attention, it can inform the prioritisation of subsequent firm-level, sectoral, and policy research without asserting outcomes that its data cannot establish.

Overall, the significance of the study rests on its capacity to connect established trade theory with a carefully qualified, product-level assessment of Bangladesh's EU market-access position. By combining observed export-market indicators with competitor comparisons, source-dated tariff benchmarks, and relevant legal conditions, the research seeks to provide an empirical foundation for understanding prospective preference erosion while retaining clear boundaries between documented exposure, hypothetical scenarios, and unobserved economic effects.

## 1.6 Scope and Brief Limitations

The scope of this study is defined by its geographical coverage, product selection, reference periods, data sources, and analytical purpose. The investigation concerns Bangladesh's existing position in the European Union (EU) import market and its prospective exposure to changes in preferential market access following eventual withdrawal from the Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement. It is designed as a product-level assessment of observed trade patterns and conditional tariff differences, rather than a forecast of the realised economic consequences of least developed country (LDC) graduation.

### 1.6.1 Scope of the Study

**Geographical and market coverage.** The study examines extra-EU merchandise imports reported for the EU-27 statistical aggregate (EU27_2020), with Bangladesh identified as the country of origin. Data are drawn primarily from Eurostat's Comext international trade in goods statistics (dataset DS-045409), which record detailed EU imports by product, partner, and reference period. In extra-EU import statistics, the partner country ordinarily denotes the country of origin rather than the country of consignment (Eurostat, 2025). Bangladesh's market position is examined alongside six selected supplying countries—China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye. These countries serve as comparative origins, not as an exhaustive representation of all suppliers. Accordingly, Bangladesh's share of a given EU product market is assessed against the official extra-EU import total for that product, rather than only the combined imports from these six comparators.

**Product coverage and units of analysis.** The principal empirical sample comprises the 200 eight-digit Combined Nomenclature (CN8) product lines with the highest reported EU import values originating in Bangladesh during 2025. Selection is based on observed import value and is therefore purposive rather than probabilistic. Although textiles and apparel are central to Bangladesh's EU trade, the sample is defined by value across the reported product universe rather than restricted in advance to a single industry. The main unit of observation is the CN8 product–origin combination for 2025, supplemented by product-level EU tariff-rate information. The analysis covers trade values, the composition and concentration of the selected Bangladeshi import basket, Bangladesh's share of extra-EU imports in the same product, competitor-country comparisons, and customs unit values where valid net-mass data permit consistent calculation. The statistical assessments address selected differences and associations within this observed product cohort; they do not independently measure exporter productivity or a comprehensive revealed comparative advantage index.

**Temporal and legal coverage.** The principal trade reference year is **2025**. The tariff comparisons use source-dated EU most-favoured-nation (MFN) and conditional preferential-rate benchmarks referenced to **1 November 2026**. These two reference dates are deliberately distinguished: the rates represent a subsequent policy benchmark applied analytically to observed 2025 imports, not duties shown to have been paid on those earlier transactions. Separate annual observations for **2023–2025** are used for historical numerical screening of the EU's textile and apparel safeguard provisions. Available data for **2012–2025** provide broader historical context at the Harmonized System two-digit (HS2) chapter level, particularly Chapters 61–63, but are not treated as a fully harmonized longitudinal CN8 product panel. This distinction is necessary because the EU's eight-digit commodity classification is revised annually (Eurostat, 2025; Baumgartner et al., 2023).

**Policy and analytical coverage.** The policy analysis focuses on the EU's Generalised Scheme of Preferences (GSP), EBA treatment, and explicitly conditional alternatives to EBA access, including illustrative MFN and other preferential-rate benchmarks where the relevant product and legal assumptions can be stated. The regulatory context takes account of Bangladesh's scheduled LDC graduation on **24 November 2029**, following the United Nations General Assembly's 2026 extension, and the separate transitional mechanism governing eventual removal from EBA under Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 (United Nations General Assembly, 2026; European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). The resulting indicative EBA transition horizon around 2032 is conditional, not an established date on which a particular future duty will become payable. The study also examines Article 33 and the relevant annexes of the 2026 EU regulation through historically dated numerical comparisons, while recognising that the safeguard mechanism has distinct denominators, legal prerequisites, and an exception for EBA beneficiaries (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

### 1.6.2 Brief Limitations of the Study

**Sample representativeness and data comparability.** The focus on the 200 highest-value CN8 products provides detail on Bangladesh's leading EU import lines but excludes smaller products and does not support unqualified generalisations to the full national export basket. The six comparator countries likewise provide selected competitive benchmarks rather than a complete census of foreign suppliers. Moreover, EU import values are recorded using an import-side valuation basis, whereas Bangladesh's export statistics may follow different valuation, timing, classification, or reporting conventions; the two sources should not be assumed to match exactly (Eurostat, 2025). Where product-level quantity observations are unavailable or non-comparable, customs unit values cannot be calculated reliably. Even when available, such unit values reflect the recorded customs value relative to net mass and cannot by themselves establish product quality, factory-gate prices, or production efficiency (Gervais, 2015).

**Classification and tariff-data constraints.** Changes in CN8 classifications can create apparent product entry, exit, or growth unless successive annual code versions are reconciled using authenticated correspondence tables. As the complete 2012–2025 CN8 concordance has not been certified for this study, long-run changes in individual CN8 lines are outside its validated inferential scope (Baumgartner et al., 2023). The tariff analysis also depends on product-specific legal conditions and on the availability of usable numerical rates. Missing or unverified duties, specific or mixed tariffs, rules-of-origin requirements, and conditional preferences cannot automatically be represented by a zero rate or by a universal ad valorem equivalent. The study therefore distinguishes qualified tariff observations from excluded or illustrative assumptions and documents the resulting analytical coverage.

**Uncertainty concerning future preferences and their use.** The tariff arrangements that will apply following an eventual EBA transition have not yet been conclusively established at the product and transaction level. Eligibility for alternative preferential schemes, origin compliance, possible safeguards, subsequent EU legal acts, and the structure of competing suppliers' preferences may change. The available data also do not establish the actual 2025 preference-utilisation rate separately for every selected CN8 product and origin. Evidence from the EU rules-of-origin literature shows why nominal preference eligibility should not be equated with universal utilisation (Sytsma, 2021). Consequently, calculations based on 2026 tariff benchmarks and fixed 2025 trade values are interpreted as **conditional static duty equivalents**, not forecasts of 2029 or 2032 customs liabilities, export losses, or welfare changes. Historical Article 33 calculations using a later beneficiary-country roster are similarly illustrative backcasts and cannot demonstrate actual or future safeguard applicability.

**Limits of statistical and causal inference.** The core product-level evidence is observational and cross-sectional, and no realised post-EBA tariff withdrawal is observed in the sample. The statistical hypotheses were developed after preliminary examination of the dataset and are treated as exploratory; their results may also be sensitive to sectoral composition and purposive product selection. Accordingly, the analysis cannot identify the causal effect of tariff-preference erosion on export volumes, prices, employment, wages, firm profitability, or economic welfare. Such outcomes would require additional behavioural assumptions or appropriately designed post-policy observations. To limit overinterpretation, the study reports source dates, valid-data coverage, missingness, sectoral and product-group sensitivities, and the boundaries between descriptive evidence, exploratory inference, and conditional legal or accounting assessments.

## 1.7 Organization of the Study

This thesis is organized into seven chapters. Chapter 1 introduces the research context, defines the problem and gap in existing knowledge, establishes the objectives and research questions, and explains the study's significance and boundaries. Chapter 2 reviews the theoretical and empirical literature on preferential market access, rules of origin, tariff preference erosion, and export competitiveness, with particular attention to Bangladesh and the European Union (EU). Chapter 3 develops the theoretical and conceptual framework, distinguishing the mechanisms suggested by trade theory from the variables observable in the study. Chapter 4 describes the research design, official data sources, product and competitor selection, measurement procedures, conditional tariff scenarios, and exploratory statistical methods, including data-quality and reproducibility safeguards. Chapter 5 presents the descriptive and statistical results, together with the relevant tables and figures, without advancing policy interpretations. Chapter 6 interprets the findings in relation to the research questions and prior scholarship, considers their implications for trade policy and exporting industries, and discusses the study's limitations and directions for further research. Finally, Chapter 7 summarizes the principal findings and contributions and provides a concluding assessment of Bangladesh's prospective EU market-access exposure. A consolidated reference list and, where permitted, supporting technical appendices follow the main chapters.
