# CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW

## 2.1 Thematic Review of Key Concepts

An assessment of Bangladesh's prospective market-access exposure in the European Union (EU) requires several concepts that are related but analytically distinct: preferential tariff treatment, tariff preference erosion, rules of origin and preference utilisation, export competitiveness, relative supplier advantages, and the measurement of exposure under uncertain future trade arrangements. These concepts are connected through international trade costs, yet they operate at different levels of analysis. A statutory tariff rate describes a condition of market access; a recorded import share describes an observed market outcome; and an estimate of how trade might respond to a future tariff change requires additional behavioural assumptions. The literature reviewed in this section is therefore organized by conceptual theme rather than by individual publication, with attention to the assumptions and limitations that matter for a product-level study of Bangladesh's exports to the EU.

### 2.1.1 Non-reciprocal Trade Preferences and the EU's Market-Access Framework

Preferential market access exists when goods from an eligible exporting country enter a destination market on tariff terms more favourable than those applicable under the otherwise relevant standard tariff schedule. In conventional non-reciprocal arrangements, the preference-granting economy reduces or removes certain import duties without requiring equivalent market-access concessions from the beneficiary. Such programmes are intended to facilitate the participation of developing economies in international trade; however, the extent of their benefits depends on product coverage, the tariffs that would apply without the preference, and the conditions governing its use (Brenton, 2003). Preferential eligibility, therefore, should not be interpreted as a guarantee of an equivalent economic advantage across all products or exporting countries.

The EU's Generalised Scheme of Preferences (GSP) is an important institutional expression of this approach. Its architecture distinguishes Standard GSP, the special incentive arrangement for sustainable development and good governance (GSP+), and Everything But Arms (EBA) for eligible least developed countries (LDCs). EBA provides duty-free and quota-free treatment for eligible imports other than arms and ammunition, subject to applicable legal conditions, including preferential origin rules. The revised legislative framework under Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 retains these distinct arrangements rather than establishing a single uniform preferential tariff for all developing-country exports (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). Consequently, any assessment of post-EBA access must differentiate the legal criteria for these arrangements instead of treating Standard GSP, GSP+, and EBA as interchangeable alternatives.

LDC graduation introduces a second institutional distinction between the date a country ceases to qualify for the United Nations' LDC category and the date its corresponding EU preferences may be withdrawn. Bangladesh's preparatory period has been extended to 24 November 2029 by the United Nations General Assembly (2026). Separately, Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 sets out the legal mechanism and further transitional period associated with removal from EBA after loss of the relevant LDC eligibility (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). These legal sequences mean that an eventual change in tariff treatment is conditional on the law and administrative decisions applicable at the time. Theoretical discussion of graduation-related preference erosion must therefore distinguish **eligibility transitions** from **immediately applicable tariff changes**.

### 2.1.2 Tariff Preference Margins, Erosion, and Relative Supplier Advantages

A tariff preference margin is commonly understood as the difference between a reference duty and the preferential duty available to a qualifying product and origin. The choice of reference rate is consequential. An MFN-based margin identifies the potential advantage relative to the general tariff, whereas a competitor-relative margin compares the rates potentially available to alternative supplying origins in the same destination market. The latter comparison is particularly relevant when several exporters compete in one product market but do not have identical access to EU preference schemes or trade agreements. Empirical research has demonstrated that estimated preferential-trade effects depend on the definition of the margin and can differ markedly by sector (Cipollina et al., 2017). Thus, it is analytically insufficient to infer an exporter's competitive advantage from its preferential rate without specifying the reference and the conditions attached to that rate.

**Tariff preference erosion** describes a reduction in an existing preferential advantage, but this reduction can occur through more than one mechanism. A beneficiary may lose or receive less favourable treatment, a decline in the MFN duty may narrow the benefit of an unchanged preference, or an improvement in the tariff treatment of competing origins may reduce the beneficiary's relative position. These mechanisms are economically distinct. For example, a reduction in the MFN duty can diminish a preference margin while simultaneously reducing the trade barrier facing the beneficiary and other exporters. Conversely, the removal of EBA treatment could raise the nominal tariff applied to a qualifying good, although the ultimate change would depend on any replacement scheme and its conditions. Analysis of EU rice imports by Raimondi et al. (2012) further illustrates that tariff-rate quotas and the construction of the reference margin can affect how preference erosion is measured. A single percentage-point difference is therefore not a universally sufficient representation of effective market access.

Preferences are also relative in a second sense: they may affect trade allocation among supplying countries. Borchert (2009) examined trade diversion associated with selective preferential access, highlighting the possibility that the advantage conferred on one group of exporters changes the competitive position of others. For Bangladesh, the economically relevant comparison is therefore not limited to a Bangladesh-versus-MFN calculation; it includes the tariff conditions under which suppliers such as China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye compete for EU import demand. These countries may be subject to different legal regimes and qualification requirements, however, so their preferential rates cannot be assigned mechanically to every shipment merely because an agreement or scheme exists. The concepts of a **statutory margin**, a **conditional competitor-relative margin**, and an **actually utilised preference advantage** must be kept separate.

### 2.1.3 Rules of Origin, Preference Utilisation, and Compliance Costs

Rules of origin (RoO) determine whether a product meets the conditions for preferential treatment under the relevant customs arrangement. Their commercial significance arises because production networks frequently span more than one country: the location of material sourcing and transformation can affect whether an exported good qualifies for a preference. The availability of a lower published tariff therefore does not imply that the preference will be claimed in every eligible product category or transaction. The benefit may be reduced by sourcing restrictions, documentation costs, certification procedures, and the administrative effort necessary to demonstrate compliance (Brenton, 2003; Sytsma, 2021). Under the revised EU GSP framework, the legal requirement for beneficiary-country origin is explicitly linked to the claiming of preferential treatment (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 39).

Research on the EU's 2011 relaxation of apparel rules of origin provides an important conceptual basis for understanding this distinction. Sytsma (2021) examined changes in preference utilisation following reforms that increased flexibility in the use of internationally sourced textile inputs, while Sytsma (2022) analysed the corresponding responses of apparel-exporting firms in Bangladesh. Together, these studies show that the value of preferential access is shaped not only by tariff schedules but also by the production and documentation conditions through which firms can use those schedules. The studies do not establish that utilisation is identical across products or that the same response would follow a future withdrawal of preferences; rather, they demonstrate why *eligibility* and *actual use* are separate empirical questions.

These distinctions have direct implications for measuring preference erosion. A statutory tariff margin establishes what a qualifying shipment **could** save relative to a specified reference duty. Actual preference utilisation establishes whether the preferential treatment was **claimed** on observed imports. The economic benefit ultimately received by exporters or importers may also depend on price negotiations and cost allocation within supply chains. Accordingly, applying an aggregate preference-utilisation rate uniformly across every CN8 product would obscure product-level heterogeneity unless supported by corresponding customs evidence. Bekkers and Cariola (2025) incorporate utilisation information into their assessment of LDC graduation, reinforcing the need to distinguish a legally available preference from its effective commercial use.

### 2.1.4 Export Competitiveness, Comparative Advantage, and Measurement

Export competitiveness is a multidimensional concept, encompassing observable success in foreign markets alongside underlying factors such as production efficiency, product characteristics, reliability, and market-access conditions. Although comparative advantage offers an influential theoretical foundation for trade specialization, its empirical representation requires careful definition. Balassa (1965) introduced a widely used revealed comparative advantage (RCA) approach based on relative patterns of exports. French (2017), however, demonstrates that RCA indicators have different interpretations and are affected by trade costs, making it inappropriate to treat any single index as a direct, context-independent measure of a country's productivity or intrinsic production advantage.

For a study based on EU import records, a product's **EU extra-EU import-market share** represents the proportion of total extra-EU imports in that CN8 category originating in Bangladesh. It is a valid destination-specific indicator of observed market position, but it is not identical to Balassa's RCA measure, which requires a suitably defined broader export benchmark. Nor does a high share, by itself, establish that a tariff preference caused the observed position: producer capabilities, buyer sourcing relationships, product mix, and the trade conditions facing rival suppliers may also be relevant. The structural gravity perspective emphasizes that bilateral trade patterns depend on trade costs in relation to alternative markets and partners (Anderson & van Wincoop, 2003). Accordingly, product-market shares and tariff margins may be considered jointly without assuming that their statistical association identifies a causal relationship.

Another commonly used indicator is the **customs unit value**, calculated by relating the declared import value to the reported quantity for a defined product and origin. Such measures can support descriptive comparisons where the product classifications and quantity units are comparable, but they do not uniquely identify quality, factory-gate prices, or production costs. Hummels and Klenow (2005) demonstrated the relevance of the variety and quality dimensions of trade to cross-country export patterns, while Gervais (2015) explicitly distinguishes product quality from other sources of firm and price heterogeneity. Recorded EUR-per-kilogram differences may reflect variation in product composition within an eight-digit category, transportation and insurance components of import valuation, or differences in the specific goods supplied. Thus, describing a lower unit value as evidence of greater productivity or inferior quality would go beyond what aggregate customs data can establish.

Finally, measures of export concentration and diversification concern a related but separate aspect of competitiveness. Concentration describes the distribution of observed trade value across products or sectors, and a highly concentrated basket may be exposed to policy or demand changes affecting those products. However, a concentration statistic constructed solely for a selected set of high-value goods characterises **that selected basket**, not necessarily the complete national export portfolio. The extent to which concentration represents vulnerability also depends on the diversity of destinations, products, buyers, and production capabilities. These distinctions matter when interpreting sector-specific tariff exposure without assuming that concentration alone predicts future export contraction.

### 2.1.5 Global Value Chains, Sourcing Structures, and Competitive Adjustment

Tariff preferences interact with the organization of production and international sourcing rather than operating in isolation from firm decisions. Global value chain (GVC) analysis emphasizes that relationships among suppliers, buyers, and lead firms are governed by differing levels of coordination and power. Gereffi et al. (2005) distinguish governance structures such as market, modular, relational, captive, and hierarchical arrangements, offering a basis for understanding why exporters may differ in their capacity to respond to changing market conditions. Within such networks, a tariff-related advantage may coexist with constraints related to inputs, compliance standards, delivery schedules, and buyer relationships; changes in any one condition need not produce a uniform response across firms.

The relevance of this perspective to Bangladesh is illustrated by the work of Curran and Nadvi (2015), who related changes in EU trade preferences and origin rules to the structure of Bangladesh's knitwear and woven-garment value chains. Their analysis supports the view that sourcing rules can interact with sector-specific production arrangements. More generally, Melitz (2003) established a theoretical framework in which firms differ in productivity and export-market participation, helping explain why a common shift in trade costs may be associated with heterogeneous adjustment across exporters. These perspectives provide plausible explanations for unequal responses to preferential-market-access changes, but product-level EU customs data cannot directly observe firm productivity, contracting terms, or the distribution of gains between buyers and producers.

The concept of **competitor-relative adjustment** is especially important for a destination such as the EU, where Bangladesh participates alongside exporters subject to a variety of tariff regimes. If the relative cost of imports from one origin rises, importers may consider alternative suppliers; whether substitution occurs, and to what extent, depends on product substitutability, supply capacity, contracts, and other commercial considerations. The trade diversion literature provides a rationale for examining these relative incentives (Borchert, 2009), while GVC research cautions against equating a tariff-rate difference with an immediate ability to replace one supplier with another (Gereffi et al., 2005). Consequently, the comparison of competing origins is conceptually necessary, but it remains distinct from observing actual supplier switching.

### 2.1.6 LDC Graduation, Conditional Exposure, and Economic Impact

Analyses of LDC graduation commonly involve a prospective change in trade preferences, but **exposure** and **impact** answer different economic questions. Exposure concerns the value of trade subject to a particular legal or tariff-rate scenario, while impact concerns the behavioural and economic responses that occur if that scenario is implemented. A static tariff-duty equivalent can be constructed by applying specified duty-rate differences to observed import values while holding the traded quantities and values unchanged. This calculation is useful for comparing the scale and product distribution of alternative tariff assumptions; however, it cannot determine the incidence of duties among importers, exporters, and consumers, nor can it establish future trade quantities or firm profitability.

The distinction is apparent in established modelling approaches. Rahman and Strutt (2024) assess Bangladesh's graduation-related market-access risks using a computable general equilibrium framework, which accounts for broader economic interactions under modelled assumptions. Bekkers and Cariola (2025) use a partial-equilibrium approach that incorporates detailed preference-utilisation information into trade simulations. These approaches estimate conditional responses, whereas a static exposure calculation identifies a duty-rate difference against a fixed recorded trade baseline. Their analytical outputs should not be compared as if an imputed customs-duty equivalent were the same quantity as a projected export decline, change in economic welfare, or reduction in gross domestic product.

The temporal and legal context further constrains interpretation. Bangladesh's scheduled UN graduation date is 24 November 2029, while the EU's separate transition rules make a prospective EBA exit around 2032 conditional on the applicable legal process and subsequent decisions (United Nations General Assembly, 2026; European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026). The relevant future product duties and preference-use conditions are therefore not established merely by observing contemporary tariff schedules. Conceptually, tariff scenarios should be labelled as **reference-date benchmarks or explicitly hypothetical post-EBA arrangements**, not as certified rates payable at an anticipated future date.

### 2.1.7 Product Disaggregation, Classification Consistency, and Safeguard Conditions

The level of product aggregation affects the interpretation of both competitiveness and tariff exposure. Sectoral totals may conceal substantial heterogeneity in tariffs, quantities, and competitor positions across goods within the same industry. The EU's Combined Nomenclature provides detailed eight-digit (CN8) classifications for merchandise trade, but these codes are revised annually (Eurostat, 2025). Baumgartner et al. (2023) show why harmonizing product classifications is necessary when developing consistent trade series over time. Without authenticated year-to-year correspondence, a change in a code's reported value may reflect reclassification rather than genuine product growth or decline. A current-year CN8 cross-section can therefore support detailed contemporaneous comparisons without automatically justifying longitudinal product-level conclusions.

Product and legal classifications are also relevant to the EU's sector-specific safeguard provisions. Under Article 33 and Annex IV of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, the special safeguard mechanism for designated products incorporates numerical criteria based on imports from GSP beneficiary origins, alongside a distinct exception for countries below a specified share of total Union imports. The regulation also excludes EBA beneficiary countries from the relevant Article 33 mechanism (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 33 and Annex IV). A historical numerical comparison with those criteria is consequently not the same as establishing legal applicability: the correct beneficiary roster, import denominator, status of the exporting country, and implementing measures all matter. This illustrates more broadly why **a threshold observed in trade data must be distinguished from a binding customs decision**.

Taken together, these themes indicate that prospective tariff preference erosion is not a single measurable event with a uniform effect on exports. It involves interactions among legal eligibility, nominal and utilised preferences, relative supplier conditions, product composition, and potential market responses. For Bangladesh's EU export position, the most defensible analytical starting point is therefore to describe product-specific market outcomes and conditional tariff differences while preserving the distinction between measured exposure and unobserved economic adjustment. The next section reviews the empirical literature comparatively, examining the methods and findings through which these conceptual relationships have previously been investigated.

## 2.2 Empirical Studies and Prior Findings

Empirical research on preferential market access has examined several interconnected but distinct outcomes: the utilisation of tariff preferences, the volume and composition of trade, firms' responses to rules-of-origin changes, and the prospective consequences of losing preferential treatment. The choice of research design matters because these outcomes cannot be treated as equivalent. Studies using customs-level observations may establish whether eligible shipments actually claim preferences; gravity models estimate associations between trade flows and trade costs under specified modelling assumptions; quasi-experimental studies exploit realised policy changes; and computable general equilibrium (CGE) or partial-equilibrium (PE) simulations estimate adjustments to hypothetical policy scenarios. This section critically compares the relevant literature by geographical setting, data and unit of observation, analytical strategy, principal findings, and limits of inference. The purpose is not to establish a single predicted loss for Bangladesh, but to determine what existing evidence can and cannot establish about differential exposure to changes in European Union (EU) market-access conditions.

### 2.2.1 EU Trade Preferences: Utilisation, Product Heterogeneity, and Trade Responses

Early assessments of the EU's Everything But Arms (EBA) initiative identified an important difference between the nominal scope of duty-free access and its incremental economic value. Brenton (2003) examined the trade-policy implications of EBA for least developed countries (LDCs) by considering the preferences offered, the underlying tariff barriers, and the practical conditions for accessing them. The analysis indicated that the potential improvement in market access was uneven: the additional nominal benefit was limited for goods already entering duty-free under the most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff, while origin requirements could restrict the effective use of available preferences. As a policy assessment rather than an evaluation of a subsequent Bangladesh-specific preference withdrawal, this research helps explain why a common preference scheme need not confer equal benefits across products or countries. It also cautions against applying a standard duty-rate assumption to all export lines.

The question of actual preference use became more explicit with the availability of detailed customs data. Using EU preferential import flows reported at the eight-digit product level for 2008, Nilsson (2011) found a pronounced difference between the number of small transactions and their contribution to trade value. More than 90% of preferential import flows by count represented only about 5% of the corresponding import value, and utilisation rates for low-value flows were markedly below the higher rates apparent in aggregate statistics. This result is particularly relevant to product-level research because an overall national utilisation ratio can be heavily influenced by a relatively small group of high-value products. Nevertheless, the study's historical period, broad developing-country coverage, and emphasis on the relationship between flow size and preference take-up do not provide contemporary Bangladesh-specific utilisation rates for individual CN8 lines. The implication is methodological: preferences **available** under customs law cannot be assumed to have been **claimed** equally across the whole product portfolio.

The wider trade-effect literature further demonstrates the importance of how preferential margins are defined. Cipollina et al. (2017) employed highly disaggregated EU eight-digit import data in a theoretically grounded gravity framework and measured tariff preferences relative to alternative reference tariffs, including an MFN benchmark and a reference linked to a constant-elasticity-of-substitution price aggregator. They estimated sector-specific trade elasticities and reported that inferences concerning preferential policies were sensitive to the definition of the margin, with substantial variation across sectors. Unlike analyses based solely on aggregate preferential eligibility, this approach places the product and the competing trade-cost environment within the empirical model. However, its estimated elasticities and policy effects pertain to the relevant model specification, trade sample, and institutional conditions; they should not be applied as universal parameters to Bangladesh's future exit from EBA. Crucially, this study also establishes that **EU eight-digit preferential-trade analysis already exists**. A Bangladesh-focused contribution must therefore be grounded in a different combination of setting, reference date, coverage, and analytical purpose rather than a claim to introduce product-level tariff research for the first time.

Related evidence has emphasized that preferential trade effects may include redistribution among suppliers and may be sensitive to sector-specific trade instruments. Borchert (2009) investigated trade diversion under selective preferential market access, drawing attention to the relative position of preferred and less-preferred suppliers rather than treating one country's preferential rate as the sole determinant of trade outcomes. In a different sectoral setting, Raimondi et al. (2012) applied a dynamic panel gravity approach to developing-country rice exports to the EU, accounting for persistence and endogeneity concerns while examining the measurement of preference margins under tariff-rate quotas. Although this agricultural framework is not directly transferable to apparel, it demonstrates why a nominal MFN-minus-preference calculation may be incomplete when quota or other product-specific instruments govern actual access. Taken together, these studies support **product- and competitor-sensitive analysis**, while also showing that conclusions depend on the chosen tariff measure, sector, estimation strategy, and legal regime.

### 2.2.2 Rules-of-Origin Reforms: Evidence from Bangladesh and Cambodia

A more direct body of evidence examines how rules of origin (RoO) affect the extent to which exporters can utilise preferential market access. The EU's 2011 reform of apparel origin requirements provides a useful realised policy change because it relaxed restrictions on the use of internationally sourced textile inputs for qualifying LDC exporters. Sytsma (2021) investigated the change using a triple-difference framework and EU preference-utilisation data, contrasting outcomes across the dimensions relevant to the reform. The study found evidence that restrictive origin conditions had materially constrained the use of trade preferences. This design offers greater leverage for assessing the effect of the specific RoO revision than a simple cross-sectional association between tariff schedules and exports. Its dependent variable, however, is preference utilisation under an implemented reform; the estimated effect cannot be read as a prediction of how Bangladesh's trade would respond to the future withdrawal of EBA.

Evidence at the firm level strengthens the interpretation that origin requirements can affect exporters through more than their statutory tariff rate. Using transaction-level data for Bangladeshi apparel firms, Sytsma (2022) analysed the same 2011 policy reform and reported that the restrictions had substantially reduced the effectively available preference margin. The paper estimated that the rules of origin effectively reduced the preferential margin by approximately three-quarters in its study context. Following liberalisation, firm revenue growth was associated principally with intensive-margin adjustments, including larger shipments and quality upgrading. These results are especially relevant because they connect trade-policy rules to observed firm responses, rather than using a sector-wide tariff average as a proxy for all commercial outcomes. Nevertheless, the direction of the historical reform was an *easing* of sourcing conditions; treating its estimated response as a symmetric elasticity for a prospective increase in tariffs or stricter post-EBA eligibility would require assumptions not established by the study.

A complementary perspective is provided by Curran and Nadvi (2015), who examined Bangladesh's textile and garment value chains through trade evidence and qualitative information concerning the policy environment and industry sourcing arrangements. Their research distinguished the experiences of knitwear and woven-garment producers and investigated how changing preferential conditions interacted with imported fabrics, domestic supply relationships, and upgrading possibilities. This study contributes institutional and production-network detail that transaction-level customs data alone may omit, particularly the influence of sourcing organization and buyer relationships. Its evidence, however, does not identify a unique causal effect of a future policy shock on firm profitability, employment, or worker welfare. It is most appropriately used to interpret potential adjustment mechanisms alongside, rather than instead of, quantitative estimates.

Cambodia offers a related but analytically separate setting. Tanaka (2021) applied a difference-in-differences design to the EU's 2011 origin-rule reform, using Cambodian garment-export evidence over **2007–2015** and examining the credibility of the parallel-trends assumption. The study reported a substantial increase in Cambodian garment exports to the EU after the reform—**112% in the reported estimate**—accompanied by increased textile sourcing from China. The result is consistent with the argument that greater freedom to use imported inputs can alter export performance and the organization of cross-border supply chains. However, Cambodia's industrial structure, exporter sample, and contemporaneous conditions differ from those of Bangladesh. Moreover, its estimated response concerns the relaxation of a specific origin rule and should not be extrapolated numerically to Bangladesh's prospective withdrawal from EBA in the 2030s.

The comparison across these studies suggests convergence on one substantive finding: **origin rules are economically meaningful constraints rather than administrative details** (Curran & Nadvi, 2015; Sytsma, 2021, 2022; Tanaka, 2021). At the same time, the magnitude of the response varies with the unit of observation, policy change, firm or country characteristics, and outcome selected. Preference utilisation, firm revenue, export value, and supply-chain restructuring remain distinct empirical outcomes. This matters for product-level exposure analysis because a source-dated legal tariff margin may describe an available incentive without observing the qualification, take-up, or behavioural adjustment through which it affects trade.

### 2.2.3 Observed Preference Withdrawal: Short-Run Adjustment and Export Redirection

Studies of the *removal* of EU preferences are particularly instructive because their policy direction is more comparable to the risk associated with graduation than evidence on origin-rule liberalisation. Tanaka (2022) analysed the partial withdrawal of EBA benefits from Cambodia in **August 2020**, when selected goods lost preferential treatment while other products remained eligible. The difference-in-differences design exploited the contrast between affected and unaffected goods, with attention to pre-intervention trends. The paper reported a statistically significant rise in EU imports of affected goods in **July 2020**, consistent with importers advancing shipments ahead of higher duties, followed by a negative effect on affected EU imports once the tariff changes took effect. Responses differed across knitted garments, woven garments, and footwear. This evidence illustrates why the timing of customs observations matters: a short pre-policy surge is not proof that the withdrawal increased underlying demand, and a single pre/post comparison may obscure shipment anticipation.

More recent evidence considers whether exporters can offset lost EU access by redirecting sales to other markets. In a **November 2026 discussion paper**, Snameas et al. (2026) used Cambodian export observations for **2017–2024** and a difference-in-differences comparison between goods affected and unaffected by the EU's 2020 withdrawal. The authors reported no statistically detectable increase in exports of affected products to non-EU destinations, while affected footwear exports declined substantially in their reported analysis. This offers a relevant, though still **non-peer-reviewed**, indication that buyer relationships and market-specific capabilities may limit short-run diversification following a preference shock. Its working-paper status, country-specific policy episode, and exposure to other contemporaneous disruptions mean that the estimates should be treated as preliminary, rather than as an established forecast for Bangladesh.

Read alongside the RoO-reform studies, the withdrawal evidence also challenges an assumption of symmetric policy effects. Easing sourcing requirements can expand the set of viable export transactions, whereas increasing duties can lead to inventory acceleration, changes in margins, lower shipments, or attempts at supplier and destination substitution. These adjustment channels do not operate in the same way or on identical timelines. Cambodia's experience therefore provides a useful *comparative analogue* for understanding possible mechanisms, but it does not deliver a transferable causal coefficient for Bangladesh's as-yet-unobserved post-EBA transition.

### 2.2.4 Modelling the Consequences of LDC Graduation: Bangladesh and Cross-Country Evidence

The literature closest to Bangladesh's graduation concern frequently uses simulation rather than exploiting an already realised loss of EBA treatment. Rahman and Strutt (2024) employed a **computable general equilibrium (CGE)** modelling framework to examine Bangladesh's prospective market-access costs under specified changes to preferential trade arrangements and related domestic policy settings. The study's modelled scenario in which developed-country import preferences are replaced with Standard GSP conditions while Bangladesh also eliminates export subsidies produced an estimated decline of approximately **0.38% in real GDP** and **6% in exports**. These are outcomes of the specified combined scenario and its calibrated economy-wide interactions; they are not directly observed declines and should not be interpreted as the isolated effect of EU tariff changes. In addition, the analysis was developed under the earlier expected graduation timetable. Its economic mechanisms remain informative, but its legal and temporal assumptions cannot be treated as automatically current following the UN's October 2026 extension.

Bekkers and Cariola (2025) offer a complementary cross-country perspective using a **partial-equilibrium simulation** informed by detailed data on actual preference utilisation. Their analysis covers graduating and non-graduating LDCs and contrasts policy scenarios in which preferences are used at observed rates with those assuming full utilisation. For the graduating countries considered collectively, their reported model projections imply adverse export changes concentrated particularly in clothing; the authors also found that assuming full utilisation would **overstate the projected adverse trade effect by about 30% relative to the actual-utilisation scenario**. The latter figure is a comparison within their model and sample, not a universal adjustment factor applicable to any tariff estimate for Bangladesh. Their results underline a significant methodological issue: a legally available tariff advantage can overstate the effective baseline advantage if some eligible imports never claimed the preference.

The differences between these two assessments are central to their interpretation. A CGE framework can represent broader production, income, and sectoral interactions under a calibrated structure, whereas a PE framework can examine tariff-related substitution and product-level or market-level responses under its particular assumptions. Neither methodology inherently supplies the exact future tariff regime, nor does either reproduce the result of an arithmetic duty calculation based on an unchanged import baseline. The choice of benchmark policy, elasticity, preference use, domestic adjustment assumptions, and set of graduating countries materially affects the resulting projections (Bekkers & Cariola, 2025; Rahman & Strutt, 2024).

This literature establishes that future market-access changes **may be economically consequential**, but not that one forecast should be accepted independently of its counterfactual design. A contemporary analysis can therefore contribute by transparently identifying the products, market shares, comparator origins, and tariff assumptions underlying *exposure* before any separate model of behavioural adjustment is applied. Conversely, such a descriptive or accounting approach should not be presented as a competing estimate of the GDP or export changes generated by CGE and PE models.

### 2.2.5 Product-Level Competitiveness: Trade Indicators, Quality, and Classification

A further empirical strand concerns how export competitiveness should be measured. French (2017) critically evaluated revealed comparative advantage indicators, showing that different indices reflect different underlying concepts and are influenced by the trade-cost environment. This work is relevant because a country's share of a specific EU CN8 import market is an observed destination-market outcome, whereas the conventional Balassa RCA index relies on a broader relative-export benchmark. The two indicators can support complementary analyses when their denominators are defined appropriately, but they cannot be interchanged without changing the object being measured.

The quality and prices literature provides a similar caution for trade-value-to-quantity measures. Hummels and Klenow (2005) showed that countries' export patterns differ across variety and quality-related dimensions, while Gervais (2015) distinguished quality from productive efficiency using a framework that draws on demand-related information rather than assuming that observed unit values represent quality directly. The implication for customs-based comparisons is straightforward: EU import value per kilogram is a useful descriptive statistic when product-year-origin records and quantities are comparable, but differences between Bangladesh and rival suppliers may also reflect product composition, transport costs, and contracting conditions. Claims about quality upgrading in firm-level studies such as Sytsma (2022) therefore should not be replicated from aggregate CN8 unit-value rankings without an appropriate quality-identification design.

Product definitions also affect the measurement of historical changes in export performance. Baumgartner et al. (2023) developed methods for harmonising changing Combined Nomenclature and production classifications over time, demonstrating why a time series that treats nominally identical or newly created codes as directly comparable can yield misleading inferences. This is a practical limitation of product-level datasets, not merely a technical presentation issue. A single-year CN8 comparison can retain detailed commodity resolution, whereas longitudinal growth rates and product-entry measures require validated cross-year correspondences for code splits, mergers, and other revisions. Consequently, it is important to distinguish **cross-sectional product detail** from **verified historical product comparability**.

These studies do not themselves estimate the consequences of Bangladesh's graduation. Their contribution is to specify what can be inferred from trade statistics and how measurement choices can shape conclusions. In particular, an empirically defensible comparison between Bangladesh and major EU suppliers must define the common product market, use an appropriate all-origin denominator for market shares, assess the comparability of reported quantities, and avoid interpreting an association between tariff candidates and market shares as proof of causation.

### 2.2.6 Comparative Appraisal of Methods, Findings, and Transferability

The literature differs substantially in the strength of inference supported by its design. Preference-use studies and detailed EU gravity estimations provide evidence about existing trade arrangements and the sensitivity of measured preferences to specification. Difference-in-differences studies of realised reforms or withdrawals use policy variation to assess particular changes under identifying assumptions, but their estimates remain specific to the products, countries, and periods observed. CGE and PE studies instead project outcomes from assumed policy scenarios and model parameters. Qualitative value-chain research provides insight into mechanisms that are not recorded in administrative trade datasets. These approaches are therefore more appropriately regarded as **complementary than mutually interchangeable**.

*Note.* The table reports the principal research approach and a selective interpretation of each study's relevance. It is not an exhaustive summary of samples, specifications, robustness tests, or full-text findings. The 2026 Cambodia discussion paper is identified separately from peer-reviewed journal evidence. Numerical predictions from different models should not be combined or compared directly without reconciling baseline years, policy scenarios, outcome definitions, and units.

Three conclusions follow from this comparison. First, research on preferences finds meaningful effects on trade and preference use, but these effects are heterogeneous across products, countries, and policy changes. Second, evidence from Bangladesh and Cambodia shows that the underlying mechanisms include sourcing flexibility, actual preference utilisation, shipment timing, and supply-chain organisation—not only the headline tariff margin. Third, prospective graduation simulations offer important conditional impact estimates, but their outputs depend on model-specific assumptions and cannot substitute for accurately dated, product-resolved information on existing market positions and tariff eligibility. These conclusions provide the empirical basis for assessing the particular research gap addressed in Section 2.3, without implying that the current study has observed a future post-EBA trade response.

## 2.3 Identification of Research Gap

The preceding review demonstrates that the economic significance of preferential market access has been examined through a substantial body of theoretical, econometric, firm-level, and policy-simulation research. Existing studies have assessed the influence of tariff preference margins on trade flows, the role of rules of origin in determining preference utilisation, firms' responses to changes in eligibility conditions, and the possible consequences of least developed country (LDC) graduation (Cipollina et al., 2017; Sytsma, 2021, 2022; Rahman & Strutt, 2024; Bekkers & Cariola, 2025). Accordingly, the problem addressed by the present research does not arise from an absence of scholarship on European Union (EU) trade preferences or Bangladesh's export challenges. Rather, it concerns the extent to which the available findings can be brought together to assess **Bangladesh's contemporary, product-specific EU market-access exposure under legally conditional and competitor-relative tariff arrangements**. The gaps identified below are therefore differences in research setting, measurement, temporal relevance, and the integration of existing analytical approaches, rather than assertions of an unexplored causal mechanism.

### 2.3.1 Geographical and Product-Level Evidence Gap

A first limitation concerns the unit of analysis and geographical specificity of previous investigations. Bangladesh-focused studies offer important but different forms of evidence. Rahman and Strutt (2024) model the potential economy-wide and sectoral consequences of changes accompanying LDC graduation, whereas Sytsma (2022) examines the effects of a historical EU rules-of-origin reform using transaction-level observations from Bangladeshi apparel exporters. Curran and Nadvi (2015) add an understanding of how preferential trading arrangements interact with knitwear and woven-garment value chains. These studies establish the importance of graduation, preferential eligibility, and production organization; however, their analytical designs are not principally intended to provide a current, consistently defined ranking of Bangladesh-origin EU imports across detailed product lines, matched to the positions of competing supplying countries and relevant tariff benchmarks.

The wider EU literature already contains detailed product-level work. In particular, Cipollina et al. (2017) analyse EU preferential trade policies using eight-digit import data and alternative measures of tariff preference margins within a gravity framework. Consequently, neither the use of CN8 data nor the examination of tariff preferences at detailed commodity levels constitutes an original methodological invention. The more defensible gap is **context-specific integration**: the studies reviewed in Sections 2.1 and 2.2 do not themselves provide a unified assessment of Bangladesh's leading **2025** EU import product lines that simultaneously reports observed import-market shares, within-basket concentration, selected competing-origin positions, and dated, conditional tariff measures. This is a gap relative to the literature examined, rather than proof that no comparable analysis exists anywhere in the wider research record.

This distinction is consequential because the composition of an export portfolio matters for assessing exposure. National totals and broad industry averages may conceal substantial variation in the value and market positions of individual product lines. A highly disaggregated Bangladesh-focused examination can reveal where the *observed trade value and nominal tariff exposure* are concentrated, without asserting that those product positions necessarily reveal intrinsic productivity or future trade performance (French, 2017). The proposed study therefore addresses a measurable product-resolution and geographic-context gap, not a missing theory of comparative advantage.

### 2.3.2 Competitor-Relative Tariff and Preference-Utilisation Gap

A second gap arises from the distinction between a beneficiary's nominal tariff advantage and its effective position relative to alternative suppliers. Brenton (2003) demonstrates that the incremental value of EBA preferences depends partly on the tariffs that would otherwise apply and the conditions under which preferences can be used. Cipollina et al. (2017) further show that the estimation of preferential trade effects is sensitive to the definition of the reference tariff, while Borchert (2009) investigates the trade-diverting implications of selective preferential access. The combined implication is that a Bangladesh-versus-MFN comparison alone cannot fully characterize Bangladesh's relative market-access position in products also supplied by countries operating under different preferential or non-preferential arrangements.

For Bangladesh's principal EU products, this creates a need to examine the same destination-product markets against a clearly specified set of competitor origins. The present study selects China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye for matched comparisons of EU import values, market shares, and customs unit values where valid quantities are available. It also considers country- and product-specific tariff-rate candidates where the legal conditions can be identified. These comparisons provide a more differentiated account of relative trade positions than a single national preference-margin figure. They nevertheless remain **selected comparator evidence**, not a census of all EU suppliers. Moreover, a preferential rate available under a bilateral agreement or GSP arrangement cannot be assumed to apply to every shipment from the corresponding country.

A further and distinct empirical limitation concerns actual preference utilisation. Nilsson (2011) documents differences in preference take-up between small and large EU import flows, while Sytsma (2021, 2022) demonstrates that origin requirements affect the extent to which preferential access is exercised. Bekkers and Cariola (2025) similarly show that assumptions about full versus actual preference utilisation can materially alter simulated graduation effects. These studies make clear that legal eligibility and observed use are not interchangeable. The present study can identify **nominal and conditional tariff advantages**, but the currently validated dataset does not contain transaction-level, product-specific evidence showing which 2025 imports actually claimed EBA or other preferential treatment. Therefore, the research responds to the gap in integrated competitor-relative *statutory exposure*, while acknowledging that the gap in fully measured **effective, utilisation-adjusted** tariff exposure remains unresolved.

### 2.3.3 Temporal and Policy-Framework Gap

The temporal relevance of the earlier graduation literature constitutes a third issue. Prospective assessments necessarily adopt the policy timetable and legal assumptions available at the time of their preparation. Rahman and Strutt (2024) and Bekkers and Cariola (2025) provide valuable modelling evidence based on defined graduation-related counterfactuals, but the legal environment subsequently changed. On **29 October 2026**, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution A/RES/81/8, extending Bangladesh's preparatory period before LDC graduation to **24 November 2029** (United Nations General Assembly, 2026). The EU also adopted **Regulation (EU) 2026/1395**, which governs the revised Generalised Scheme of Preferences (GSP) and provides a further transitional mechanism for the eventual removal of a country from the Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement following loss of LDC eligibility (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 17).

The importance of these developments is not simply that they change a date in the background narrative. Graduation from the UN category and withdrawal of EU EBA treatment are legally separate events; following the currently defined three-year EU transition, the indicative EBA exit horizon would arise around **November 2032**, subject to the applicable legal process and subsequent decisions. This does not establish a future date on which a particular tariff will necessarily become payable. Nor does it establish Bangladesh's eligibility for Standard GSP, GSP+, or an alternative arrangement at that time. A current exposure assessment therefore requires explicit differentiation between **2025 observed trade**, **2026 source-dated tariff benchmarks**, and **future conditional legal scenarios**, rather than retrospectively treating earlier assumed graduation or preference-withdrawal dates as binding facts.

The contribution of the present study in this dimension is to integrate its trade-product analysis with the legal framework as documented at the research reference date. This improves the interpretability of alternative tariff scenarios while preserving the unavoidable uncertainty of future eligibility rules, delegated acts, product coverage, rules of origin, and trade agreements. It does **not** amount to a prediction of the precise tariff regime that Bangladesh will face after the EBA transition.

### 2.3.4 Measurement and Methodological Transparency Gap

A fourth issue relates to the measurement choices that underpin product-level competitiveness analysis. Existing research establishes that apparently simple trade indicators can be misinterpreted when their underlying denominators or identifying assumptions are not made explicit. French (2017), for example, demonstrates that revealed comparative advantage indicators are not invariant measures of productivity or intrinsic competitiveness. An EU product-market share, calculated using imports from all extra-EU origins in the same category, is consequently a destination-specific outcome measure rather than a conventional world-benchmark Balassa RCA index. Likewise, Gervais (2015) shows why quality and firm heterogeneity cannot be inferred uniquely from price-related observations; a customs value-per-kilogram comparison does not by itself identify product quality, production costs, or exporter efficiency.

Classification is another important limitation. The EU's eight-digit Combined Nomenclature is subject to annual revision, and Baumgartner et al. (2023) document the need to reconcile changes in commodity classifications when constructing consistent historical trade series. A set of nominal CN8 codes observed over multiple years cannot automatically be treated as the same set of economically comparable product categories in each year. Without authenticated annual correspondence tables, apparent growth or disappearance of a particular code may be a classification artefact. Accordingly, the present study takes **2025 as the principal CN8 cross-section**, uses historical HS2 aggregates only where they can be interpreted consistently, and does not claim to have established a fully harmonized 2012–2025 CN8 panel.

The methodological gap relevant to this project is therefore not an absence of sophisticated statistical or classification techniques in the literature. It is the need for an **auditable implementation** that keeps separate the underlying trade values, the product and origin denominators used in market-share calculations, the rates that are numerically and legally qualified, and observations excluded for missing or ambiguous information. The study's deliberately selected top-200 high-value cohort also restricts the generalizability of statistical tests to broader product populations. Its exploratory associations can support description and sensitivity analysis but cannot identify the causal impact of a future preference withdrawal. These boundaries are integral to the contribution: a transparent measurement framework is preferable to greater apparent precision built on unsupported assumptions.

### 2.3.5 Legal-Applicability and Safeguard-Interpretation Gap

An additional policy-specific gap concerns the interpretation of the EU's textile and apparel safeguard provisions. Assessments that focus only on a country's import share may overlook the importance of the legal definition of the relevant comparison group and the conditions under which the competent authority may act. Under **Article 33 and Annex IV of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395**, the relevant numerical safeguard screen for the designated textile and apparel section distinguishes a threshold measured against imports from applicable **GSP beneficiary countries** from a separate exception based on a country's share of **total Union imports** of the same products. The 37% and 6% provisions therefore involve different denominators. The regulation also exempts EBA beneficiary countries from this Article 33 safeguard mechanism while they remain within that arrangement (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026, art. 33 and Annex IV).

This legal distinction matters because the appearance of a numerical threshold in historical trade data does not, on its own, establish that a safeguard has applied or will apply to Bangladesh. The relevant annual list of beneficiary countries, product classification, implementing acts, and Bangladesh's own legal status must be established before any conclusion about enforceable duties is possible. The present study adds a **carefully labelled historical numerical screening exercise** for HS Chapters 61–63, including an explicit beneficiary-roster backcast, while separating that exercise from any claim of actual legal applicability. The resulting contribution is to the accuracy and transparency of tariff-risk interpretation, rather than to the estimation of an imposed safeguard or a prediction of future Commission decisions.

### 2.3.6 Synthesis of the Research Gap and Contribution of the Present Study

The literature reviewed above makes it possible to specify the research contribution without overstating its originality. Existing studies provide important knowledge about the economic and institutional operation of preferences, the consequences of origin-rule changes, and modelled outcomes following LDC graduation. What the present study adds is an **integrated, source-dated and reproducible product-level assessment** of Bangladesh's leading EU import lines under a set of clearly stated contemporary and hypothetical tariff conditions. Its research contribution lies chiefly in linking existing constructs and data sources within one Bangladesh-focused analytical framework.

**Table 2.2. Research gaps identified in the reviewed literature and the scope of the present study**

| Dimension | What the existing research establishes | Contribution of the present study | Remaining boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Geographical and product resolution | Bangladesh-specific CGE, firm-level and value-chain studies; EU CN8 preference research already exists (Curran & Nadvi, 2015; Sytsma, 2022; Cipollina et al., 2017; Rahman & Strutt, 2024) | Examines the 200 leading 2025 Bangladesh-origin CN8 import lines in the EU and their product-level market positions | Selected high-value cohort; not every Bangladesh export line |

*Note.* The table identifies gaps **relative to the studies examined**, not the verified absence of any comparable research in the global literature. “Contribution” denotes the analytical scope supported by the present study's source-defined data and methods, not an assertion that it already measures the economic effects of an unobserved future policy change.

The study's empirical design follows from this synthesis. First, it examines concentration and observed EU import-market positions within the selected 2025 CN8 product cohort. Second, it compares Bangladesh's product-level position with six competing origins using trade values, shares, and valid customs unit values. Third, it examines nominal and conditional competitor-relative tariff differences using source-dated rates and stated qualifications. Fourth, it calculates fixed-trade-value duty equivalents for alternative tariff scenarios, explicitly excluding the inference that these accounting results measure export losses. Finally, it evaluates the numerical provisions of the EU's product-specific safeguard framework using precisely specified import denominators and historical beneficiary-roster assumptions. These components correspond to the study's five research objectives and the research questions stated in Chapter 1.

The central research gap can thus be expressed as follows: **within the empirical and legal literature reviewed, there remains a need for an up-to-date, Bangladesh-specific assessment that integrates detailed EU product-market positions, selected competitor-origin comparisons, dated tariff-preference benchmarks, alternative post-EBA exposure scenarios, and the legal qualifications governing those scenarios in one reproducible analytical framework.** The present study addresses that integrated measurement problem. It does not claim to identify the causal effect of tariff preference erosion, calculate future export or welfare losses, measure firm-level preference utilisation, or establish a harmonized historical CN8 product panel. Preserving these distinctions allows the study to contribute to a more precise understanding of prospective market-access exposure without treating conditional scenarios as realised economic outcomes.
