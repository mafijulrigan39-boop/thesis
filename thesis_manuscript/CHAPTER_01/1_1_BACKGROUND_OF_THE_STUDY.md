# CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION

## 1.1 Background of the Study

International trade provides opportunities for countries to expand production, access larger markets, and participate in cross-border networks of specialization. However, the ability to compete in a foreign market depends not only on productive capacity but also on the costs and institutional conditions associated with reaching that market. Import tariffs alter the relative costs of products supplied from different origins, while trade policy arrangements can influence the distribution of market-access advantages across countries. The structural gravity literature identifies bilateral trade costs and conditions in alternative supplying markets as important determinants of international trade patterns (Anderson & van Wincoop, 2003). For developing economies whose export industries depend heavily on a limited number of overseas markets, the terms of preferential market access are therefore an important component of their external trading environment.

Non-reciprocal trade preferences have long formed part of the international trade and development framework. Under such arrangements, preference-granting economies offer eligible developing-country exports reduced or zero import duties without requiring equivalent concessions in return. Their rationale is to improve market-access opportunities for economies facing structural development constraints. Nevertheless, the economic value of a preference depends on the tariff that would otherwise apply, the products covered, and the administrative conditions for claiming the benefit. Brenton (2003) demonstrated that the usefulness of the European Union's (EU) Everything But Arms (EBA) arrangement varies across least developed countries (LDCs), partly because some products already enter the EU at zero most-favoured-nation (MFN) duties and because compliance with origin requirements may limit use of available preferences.

The concept of *tariff preference erosion* refers to a weakening of the relative tariff advantage enjoyed by a preferred exporter. Such erosion can arise through the removal or modification of a beneficiary's preferential status, reductions in MFN tariffs that narrow the difference between preferential and standard rates, or improvements in the preferential access available to competing suppliers. Consequently, the commercial significance of tariff preferences cannot be assessed by considering a beneficiary country's duty rate in isolation. It also depends on the tariff treatment of competing origins and on the sensitivity of trade to differences in effective market-access costs. Research using detailed EU import data has shown that estimated effects of preferential trade policies vary across sectors and are sensitive to how the relevant preference margins are defined (Cipollina et al., 2017).

The EU provides a particularly important institutional setting in which to examine these issues. Its Generalised Scheme of Preferences (GSP) differentiates between Standard GSP, the special incentive arrangement for sustainable development and good governance (GSP+), and EBA for countries classified by the United Nations as LDCs. EBA offers duty-free and quota-free access to the EU market for eligible products other than arms and ammunition, subject to the scheme's applicable conditions, including rules of origin. In contrast, the tariff treatment available through other GSP arrangements is conditional on the relevant product coverage, beneficiary eligibility, and regulatory requirements. The EU adopted Regulation (EU) 2026/1395 to govern its revised preferential framework from 1 January 2027, maintaining the distinction between these arrangements while updating the applicable institutional rules (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

Importantly, preferential eligibility does not necessarily mean that an exporter obtains preferential treatment in every transaction. Rules of origin determine whether the production and sourcing of a product satisfy the conditions attached to the preference, while documentary and administrative requirements affect whether the preference is claimed. Evidence from the EU's 2011 reform of origin rules for LDC apparel exports demonstrates that such conditions can materially influence preference utilisation (Sytsma, 2021). Using transaction-level data from Bangladeshi apparel exporters, Sytsma (2022) also documented how more flexible origin rules were associated with changes in exporter performance. These studies establish an important distinction between the *nominal availability* of preferential tariffs and their *effective utilisation* in international trade.

The eventual graduation of a country from the UN's LDC category introduces an additional dimension of market-access uncertainty. Although graduation recognises progress against international development criteria, preferences that explicitly depend on LDC status are not necessarily permanent. At the same time, loss of UN LDC classification and withdrawal of EBA treatment do not necessarily occur on the same date. On 29 October 2026, the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution A/RES/81/8, extending Bangladesh's preparatory period before graduation until **24 November 2029** (United Nations General Assembly, 2026). Under Article 17 of Regulation (EU) 2026/1395, removal from EBA following loss of LDC eligibility involves a further three-year transitional period and a delegated legal act. Accordingly, if the current timetable and regulatory arrangements remain unchanged, the relevant EU preference-transition horizon would extend approximately to **November 2032**, rather than ending automatically on Bangladesh's UN graduation date. The exact future application date and the replacement tariff regime remain dependent on the legal measures and eligibility conditions in force at that time (European Parliament & Council of the European Union, 2026).

These international arrangements are especially significant for Bangladesh because its export relationship with the EU is both substantial and concentrated in a limited range of manufactured products. According to the European Commission, bilateral EU–Bangladesh merchandise trade reached approximately **€23.3 billion in 2025**, and textiles accounted for **almost 94% of the value of EU merchandise imports from Bangladesh** in that year (European Commission, n.d.). The importance of preferential access is also evident in customs utilisation data: the European Commission and the High Representative of the Union for Foreign Affairs and Security Policy (2026) reported that approximately **€19 billion** of EU imports from Bangladesh used EBA preferences in **2024**, corresponding to a preference-utilisation rate of **96%** among eligible imports. The distinction between these different statistical indicators is important: total bilateral trade, EU import values, and the value of imports claiming EBA treatment refer to different measures and should not be interpreted interchangeably.

Bangladesh's export dependence on apparel reflects a longer process of integration into international production and sourcing networks. The evolution of its knitwear and woven-garment industries has been influenced by both access to European demand and the specific rules governing preferential origin. Curran and Nadvi (2015) showed how changes in EU origin rules interacted with the sourcing structure and upgrading prospects of Bangladesh's textile and garment value chains. This experience suggests that changes in preferential market access may have implications beyond customs duties alone, potentially shaping purchasing decisions, fabric-sourcing arrangements, and the allocation of activities within production networks. However, those mechanisms need to be distinguished from the observable trade outcomes available in aggregate merchandise statistics.

The prospective change in Bangladesh's EU market-access conditions also raises questions concerning its position relative to suppliers facing different tariff and trade-agreement arrangements. Exporters from Bangladesh do not compete against a single uniform benchmark: competing origins may face MFN duties, GSP-based preferences, bilateral agreement rates, or other product-specific customs conditions. An identical change in Bangladesh's nominal tariff treatment could therefore have differing commercial implications across product categories and competing supply origins. Moreover, competitiveness is multidimensional. Trade-share measures can describe observed patterns of export specialization, but they do not by themselves establish underlying productivity or the causal effect of a tariff preference (Balassa, 1965; French, 2017). A distinction is therefore needed between observed export performance, relative tariff treatment, and the potential consequences of future policy changes.

Existing economic assessments of LDC graduation indicate why this distinction matters. Rahman and Strutt (2024) examined possible consequences of Bangladesh's graduation through a computable general equilibrium framework, while Bekkers and Cariola (2025) analysed graduation-related changes in trade using a partial-equilibrium framework that incorporated actual preference utilisation. Such studies provide valuable evidence on the possible channels through which policy changes may influence trade. Nevertheless, model-based projections depend on their specified tariff scenarios, behavioural assumptions, and other economic conditions. They should not be conflated with the direct arithmetic of applying an alternative tariff rate to an unchanged base of observed imports.

For an assessment of Bangladesh's exposure, the product dimension is consequently important. The value of a preferential arrangement depends on the particular goods exported, the applicable tariff schedule, the extent to which preference conditions are fulfilled, and the market positions of rival suppliers. Broad merchandise or sector-level aggregates may obscure important differences between products within the same industry. The EU's Combined Nomenclature provides detailed eight-digit commodity classifications for reporting merchandise trade by product and origin; Eurostat also notes that these classifications are revised over time, a feature that must be respected when interpreting product-level trade patterns (Eurostat, 2025).

Against this background, Bangladesh's approaching transition from LDC-based preferential access presents a question of **differentiated exposure rather than a uniform and predetermined export loss**. It is necessary to establish how the country's EU export basket is distributed across products, which goods currently benefit from meaningful preferential tariff differences, how competing suppliers are positioned in the same EU product markets, and how alternative legally conditional market-access arrangements could change those differences. Addressing these questions through clearly defined product-level evidence can contribute to a more precise understanding of Bangladesh's prospective EU market-access vulnerability and provide a grounded basis for subsequent analysis of export competitiveness and trade-policy options.
