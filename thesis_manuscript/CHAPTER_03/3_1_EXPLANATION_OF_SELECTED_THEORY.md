# CHAPTER 3: THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

## 3.1 Explanation of Selected Theory

The theoretical framework of this study is guided primarily by the **trade-cost and preferential market-access perspective**, supported by concepts of **comparative advantage and observed export competitiveness**. Global value chain (GVC) governance and heterogeneous-firm trade theory provide a secondary interpretive perspective on why exporters may respond differently to changes in trade conditions. This selection reflects the specific analytical question: how Bangladesh's present export position in the European Union (EU) is distributed across products and competing suppliers, and how the removal or modification of tariff preferences could alter its *conditional* market-access advantage. The study does not seek to explain foreign direct investment location or to estimate firms' actual adjustment to a preference withdrawal that has not yet taken place. Consequently, the framework privileges theories that explain differences in international trade costs, relative market access, and observable product-market outcomes, while distinguishing those theoretical mechanisms from what the available data can demonstrate.

### 3.1.1 Trade-Cost Theory and Structural Gravity: The Primary Foundation

The central premise of international trade-cost theory is that the pattern of trade depends not only on production and demand but also on the costs of supplying a particular destination relative to other possible sources of supply. These costs can include transport, information, and administrative costs, as well as customs tariffs and other trade-policy restrictions. In the structural gravity framework, bilateral trade is shaped by the economic characteristics of trading partners and by **multilateral resistance**—the trade-cost conditions faced in relation to alternative markets and suppliers (Anderson & van Wincoop, 2003). Accordingly, an importer's demand for goods from Bangladesh cannot be understood solely by reference to the tariff faced by Bangladesh; the conditions confronting other exporters to the same destination are also relevant.

Anderson and van Wincoop (2003) provide an important theoretical basis for this relational interpretation of market access. Their contribution is not simply that greater bilateral trade costs tend to discourage trade, but that the influence of a particular trade barrier must be understood within the broader configuration of trade costs. For the present research, this implies that a preferential tariff can have different competitive meanings across EU product markets. A low Bangladesh-specific duty may create a relatively large advantage where rival origins face higher barriers, but a smaller one where rivals also qualify for similar treatment or where the standard most-favoured-nation (MFN) tariff is already zero. The *relative* tariff environment is thus the theoretically relevant context for comparing supplying origins, even when only a subset of all suppliers is selected for detailed descriptive analysis.

An important methodological qualification follows from this theoretical foundation. A structural gravity **theory** can motivate product- and origin-specific comparisons without requiring the study to estimate a structural gravity **model**. Such estimation ordinarily involves an appropriate set of origin–destination trade flows, trade-cost variation, and an empirically defensible specification of other determinants. This thesis instead relies primarily on a 2025 EU import cross-section for selected products and supplying origins, together with a separately dated 2026 tariff reference. These observations do not contain a realised Bangladeshi withdrawal from EBA or the variation needed to identify its causal consequences. Therefore, structural gravity is used as the rationale for considering relative market access, **not** as a claim that the analysis estimates gravity elasticities or the causal effect of future preference erosion. EU product-market shares and tariff differences remain observed or constructed indicators rather than estimated structural parameters.

### 3.1.2 Preferential Market Access, Tariff Margins, and Trade Diversion

Preferential market-access analysis applies the trade-cost perspective to tariffs that differ according to the origin and eligibility of a good. A tariff preference changes the statutory conditions under which an eligible good may enter an importing market, potentially reducing its landed cost relative to goods from less-preferred suppliers. The **nominal preference margin** is commonly described as the difference between a specified reference tariff and the lower tariff legally available to a qualifying product and origin. Brenton (2003), in an early assessment of the EU's Everything But Arms (EBA) initiative, emphasizes why the incremental market-access value of EBA can vary with the underlying MFN tariff and the conditions attached to preferential treatment. If the MFN rate is zero, a zero preferential rate provides no nominal MFN-based tariff advantage; if the MFN rate is positive, a preferential rate may provide a margin, subject to origin and other customs requirements.

The interpretation becomes more complex when several exporting countries face different policy arrangements. Research on preferential trade diversion highlights that access granted to one origin can alter the relative attractiveness of other origins (Borchert, 2009). Accordingly, the erosion of Bangladesh's preference advantage need not occur only through an increase in its own tariff. The advantage can also narrow if tariffs on competing suppliers fall or if the reference MFN duty is liberalised. These alternatives have different implications for total import costs and the distribution of benefits among origins. Cipollina et al. (2017), using disaggregated eight-digit EU import data in a theoretically grounded gravity setting, show that estimated effects of preferential trade policies vary by sector and depend on the measure of the preferential margin. Their findings support distinguishing an MFN-reference margin from a competitor-relative measure rather than adopting one universal percentage-point advantage across all products.

For the present study, these insights justify considering the tariff position of Bangladesh alongside six selected competitors—China, India, Viet Nam, Pakistan, Cambodia, and Türkiye—in matched EU product markets. Importantly, the comparison must distinguish **potential legal eligibility** from **actual preferential use**. Sytsma (2021) demonstrates that rules of origin affect preference utilisation, while the Bangladesh firm-level evidence of Sytsma (2022) shows that origin-rule liberalisation can influence exporters' adjustments. These findings caution against assuming that every legally preferential rate was claimed on every eligible shipment. Similarly, the applicability of a country's trade agreement, cumulation rule, or preferential scheme cannot be inferred from country identity alone. The theory explains why such conditions are economically relevant; the empirical assessment must nevertheless label rates as conditional when product- and transaction-specific compliance is unobserved.

A final distinction concerns the meaning of **tariff preference erosion**. The loss of a nominal margin describes a potential change in an incentive or trade-cost advantage, but does not itself specify whether the burden would fall on exporters, importers, or consumers. Nor does it identify the quantity response, substitution towards competitors, or changes in production decisions. Rahman and Strutt (2024) and Bekkers and Cariola (2025) use different economy-wide and partial-equilibrium simulation frameworks to explore such prospective effects under explicitly specified assumptions. Their modelled trade or welfare adjustments are conceptually different from a calculation that applies alternative tariff rates to unchanged observed imports. For the present research, **static tariff exposure is an accounting construct grounded in preferential market-access theory**, not a theoretical or empirical estimate of realised export losses.

### 3.1.3 Comparative Advantage and Destination-Specific Export Competitiveness

Comparative advantage provides a complementary perspective on why economies specialise in different goods and participate unequally in foreign markets. Its empirical interpretation, however, is not straightforward. Balassa (1965) developed a widely used revealed comparative advantage (RCA) indicator based on the relative composition of observed exports. Such measures have been influential in empirical trade research because specialization can be examined using available trade statistics. Nevertheless, French (2017) demonstrates that RCA measures differ in their theoretical meaning and can be affected by trade frictions. They should therefore not be interpreted automatically as direct indicators of productive efficiency or as invariant rankings of countries' underlying cost advantages.

This distinction is particularly important for a study examining one destination market. The proportion of the EU's extra-EU imports of a specific product originating in Bangladesh is a **destination-product market-share indicator**. It is meaningful evidence of Bangladesh's recorded position in that product market, but it is not the conventional Balassa RCA index, which requires a broader and consistently defined export comparison. Moreover, a relatively high market share may reflect several factors—including the composition of demand, established buyer relationships, preferential tariffs, product differentiation, and supply conditions—rather than a single source of competitiveness. Thus, observed market share should be interpreted as an **outcome that may be consistent with several mechanisms**, not as proof that tariff preferences created the outcome.

Two additional dimensions qualify the interpretation of observed competitiveness. First, the concentration of trade value across products describes the structure of the selected export basket, which may be important when exposure to a particular trade-policy change is uneven across sectors. A concentration index is nevertheless conditional on the products included and does not establish the complete national level of export diversification when only high-value lines are selected. Second, customs unit values derived from import value and valid quantity observations may provide a descriptive basis for comparing matched origins but are not direct measures of quality or technical productivity. Gervais (2015) distinguishes product quality from other sources of price and firm heterogeneity, demonstrating the need for additional evidence before interpreting price-related differences as quality advantages. In this thesis, the relevance of comparative-advantage and competitiveness research therefore lies in **defining the observable indicators appropriately and recognizing their limitations**, rather than in estimating latent exporter productivity or assuming that lower EUR-per-kilogram values establish greater efficiency.

### 3.1.4 Global Value Chain Governance and Heterogeneous Firms: Supporting Perspectives

While tariff and comparative-market-access concepts provide the principal theoretical basis for this study, they do not explain every commercial adjustment through which changes in tariffs might affect exporters. The GVC perspective offers a complementary account of how the organization and governance of cross-border production can influence firms' capacity to respond. Gereffi et al. (2005) identify five forms of value-chain governance—market, modular, relational, captive, and hierarchy—associated with differences in transaction complexity, the ability to codify requirements, and supplier capabilities. These forms of organization help explain why exporters may face different constraints in sourcing inputs, meeting buyer requirements, or shifting between customers when the terms of international trade change.

The relevance to Bangladesh is supported by Curran and Nadvi (2015), who examine how EU preference arrangements and rules of origin interacted with the country's knitwear and woven-garment value chains. Their analysis indicates that trade-policy incentives cannot be separated entirely from sourcing structures and industry organization. In addition, Melitz (2003) provides a theoretical explanation for why firms with heterogeneous productivity and export-market participation may respond differently to changes in trade costs. Such perspectives offer plausible mechanisms for uneven adjustment across firms and industries even when they face the same statutory tariff rate.

Their role in the present framework is **interpretive rather than directly testable**. Aggregate EU product–origin import records do not identify individual exporters, supplier–buyer contracts, ownership structures, production costs, or the distribution of tariff burdens along a value chain. Accordingly, the study cannot classify Bangladeshi firms by GVC governance type, verify firm-specific productivity responses, or estimate how future tariff changes will be transmitted through purchasing agreements. These theories inform the discussion of *why* behavioural responses could differ across products and suppliers; they do not convert the observed product-level associations into verified firm-level causal mechanisms.

### 3.1.5 Rationale for Theoretical Selection and Boundaries of Inference

The preceding discussion leads to a deliberately layered theoretical framework. **Trade-cost and preferential-market-access theory** is selected as the primary lens because the research question concerns how origin- and product-specific tariff conditions alter the structure of relative access to one destination market. The **comparative-advantage and competitiveness literature** provides a secondary lens for interpreting observed EU import shares, product concentration, and customs unit values without conflating those indicators with productivity. **GVC governance and heterogeneous-firm theory** are retained as contextual explanations for potential differences in exporter adjustment, but not as directly estimated mechanisms. This hierarchy is consistent with the evidence reviewed in Chapter 2 and with the observational design outlined for the empirical study.

Other theories that might be appropriate for different international business questions are not introduced as independent explanatory foundations solely for comprehensiveness. In particular, the ownership–location–internalisation (OLI) framework principally addresses the international production and investment choices of firms. Because this research does not analyse foreign direct investment location, multinational ownership decisions, or the internalisation of cross-border activities, using OLI as the principal theoretical framework would not directly explain its measured tariff and trade variables. Institutional conditions remain relevant insofar as they determine legally available preference regimes and rules of origin, but this study does not separately estimate the effects of institutional quality or institutional change.

The theoretical argument also separates **three levels of inference**. First, trade theory indicates that tariffs and relative access conditions can influence trade incentives; these are economic mechanisms established in the literature. Second, the study can measure the observed 2025 distribution of EU imports and compare it with source-dated, legally qualified 2026 tariff benchmarks. Third, alternative post-EBA rates can be applied to those recorded trade values to construct **conditional static duty equivalents**. The third level is a hypothetical accounting exercise, not an observation of a future trade-policy shock or an estimate of exporters' behavioural responses. Questions about the eventual tariff incidence, demand elasticities, destination switching, firm upgrading, and welfare consequences remain outside the identified empirical design.

Thus, the selected theoretical foundations support a coherent but bounded explanation of Bangladesh's prospective EU market-access exposure: preferential treatment affects the *conditions* of competition, observed trade statistics describe the *existing distribution* of market participation, and trade-policy changes may generate *responses* that require evidence or models beyond the present cross-section to estimate. Section 3.2 applies these foundations explicitly to the study's measured variables and constructed scenario indicators; Section 3.3 then summarizes the proposed conceptual relationships, with arrows understood as analytical or theoretically motivated links rather than causal effects established by the data.
